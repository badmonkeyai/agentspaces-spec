## 1. Vision

AgentSpaces is a peer-to-peer suite of services that lets autonomous agents, including LLM-driven agents, robots, sensor nodes, and conventional services, discover each other, form scoped peer groups, and coordinate work through a shared, replicated, associative memory. Fleets of heterogeneous agents thus gain discovery, teaming, and tasking with no central broker, and operators gain a coordination fabric that keeps working through partitions and churn. The design fuses the two strongest ideas of the early-2000s Java distributed-computing stack and upgrades their weak points with the lessons of the intervening twenty years.

From JXTA, AgentSpaces takes the peering model: stable cryptographic peer identity independent of network location, peer groups as scoped trust and discovery domains, advertisements as the universal self-describing discovery currency, and a topology that tolerates NAT, churn, and heterogeneous transports through rendezvous and relay roles.

From Jini and JavaSpaces, AgentSpaces takes the coordination model: a typed tuple space with `write`, `read`, and `take` operations and template matching, leases on all state so that nothing lives forever without an owner renewing it, event notification for reactive patterns, and the replicated-worker "bag of tasks" as the canonical tasking idiom.

The upgrades draw from the modern P2P canon. Gossip protocols (SWIM-style membership, epidemic dissemination, push-sum aggregation) replace JXTA's loosely consistent rendezvous walker as the dissemination layer. CRDT replication replaces the centralized space implementation, which makes the tuple space partition-tolerant and offline-capable. Content addressing handles bulk payloads. For exclusivity on `take`, JavaSpaces relied on a global transaction monitor; AgentSpaces instead offers a spectrum of conflict-resolution strategies, from lease-based deterministic arbitration to CBBA-style probabilistic auction-consensus, selectable per space.

Finally, AgentSpaces is capability-extensible. Gossip learning, decentralized aggregation, voting, auction-based allocation, and future conversational agreement services all take the form of capability services that peers advertise, discover, and bind to through the same advertisement machinery the core uses for everything else. The core stays small: identity, peering, advertisements, and the space.

The first delivery target is an Embabel extension (`embabel-agentspaces`). Embabel's Spring-native annotation model (`@Agent`, `@Action`, `@Goal`, `@Condition`), its typed domain-object blackboard, and its API/SPI split give AgentSpaces both a natural host and a design language. An AgentSpace generalizes Embabel's per-process blackboard into a shared, leased, peer-to-peer blackboard spanning many agents on many machines, and Embabel agent metadata (descriptions, goals, input/output types) supplies exactly the raw material from which the extension generates capability advertisements automatically.

## 2. Design Principles

**P1: The weakest sufficient primitive.** AgentSpaces treats consensus as a scarce resource. Default semantics are eventually consistent (gossip plus CRDTs), exclusivity is lease-scoped, and total ordering is available only as an opt-in capability service. Every API that offers a stronger guarantee names its cost.

**P2: Everything is leased.** All shared state, including entries, advertisements, group memberships, capability bindings, and subscriptions, carries a TTL and vanishes unless its owner renews it. Failure handling therefore reduces, in most cases, to the simple absence of renewal. AgentSpaces inherits this semantic from Jini, and it is the single most important inheritance in the design.

**P3: Everything is a signed advertisement.** Typed, signed, TTL-bearing documents describe peers, groups, spaces, capabilities, and services. Discovery, trust, and binding all operate on advertisements. JXTA supplied this insight; AgentSpaces makes the signatures mandatory.

**P4: Groups are the unit of everything.** Each peer group defines its own membership policy, gossip scope, space replication scope, and capability visibility. A peer participates in many groups, and a deployment's bootstrap group, when it defines one (§5.1), is simply the outermost.

**P5: Typed entries, domain-first.** Following Embabel's domain-model philosophy, space entries are plain Java records or POJOs, matched by type plus template and serialized with a stable schema. The core API accepts no stringly-typed blobs.

**P6: Small core, advertised extensions.** The core normative surface is Layers 0 through 3 (§4 through §7). Learning, voting, auctions, ordering, and every other extension arrive as capability services (Layer 4), each with its own advertisement type and binding handshake.

**P7: Local-first, transport-agnostic.** A node with zero connectivity still works against its local replicas, and synchronization proceeds opportunistically. Transports (TCP, QUIC, WebSocket, Zenoh/DDS bridges, even sneakernet sync) sit behind an SPI, so the choice of transport remains orthogonal to the coordination semantics above it.

## 3. Architecture Overview

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Layer 4  CAPABILITY SERVICES (advertised, pluggable)            │
│   task-auction (CBBA)  ·  vote  ·  aggregate (push-sum)         │
│   gossip-learn  ·  ordered-log (Raft quorum)  ·  custom…        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Layer 3  AGENTSPACE (distributed tuple space)                   │
│   write/read/take/notify · templates · leases · CRDT replication│
│   conflict-resolution strategies · content-addressed payloads   │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Layer 2  ADVERTISEMENT & DISCOVERY                              │
│   typed signed documents · TTL · local ad-cache · queries       │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Layer 1  PEERING                                                │
│   peer groups · SWIM membership · gossip dissemination          │
```

Each layer depends only on the layers below it. A conforming minimal implementation comprises Layers 0 through 3 on a single LAN with the default conflict strategy; capability services and WAN topology are additive.

## 4. Layer 0: Identity

**4.1 PeerID.** Every peer generates an Ed25519 (RFC 8032) keypair on first start and derives `PeerID = multibase(sha-256(public-key))`. The ID stays stable across restarts, addresses, and transports, because the peer persists it in its keystore. The originating peer signs all protocol messages and all advertisements with its key, and receivers MUST verify signatures before caching or acting.

**4.2 AgentID.** A peer may host multiple logical agents, as an Embabel application typically does. `AgentID = (PeerID, local-name)`. Agents inherit the peer's key for transport-level signing; an agent MAY additionally carry its own subordinate Ed25519 keypair, certified by the peer key, when per-agent non-repudiation matters (v0.1.11, QA4 A4-7 phase 1). The certificate is `AgentCertificate = (agent, agentPublicKey, issued, ttl, peerSignature, encryptionPublicKey?)`, where the optional X25519 `encryptionPublicKey` (v0.1.13) is appended and omitted when absent (§11a.2a): canonical CBOR, the peer's signature covering the record minus the signature field, valid from `issued` until `issued + ttl` exclusive, and issuable only by the peer the AgentID names. A record written under a subordinate identity is signed by the agent key and travels with the certificate beside the peer key (§7.1, §11a.4); a receiver MUST verify the certificate under the peer key, require it to name exactly the record's issuer and to cover the record's signing time — its issue stamp's physical time must lie in `[issued, issued + ttl)` and may lead the receiver's clock by at most the bounded HLC drift (v0.1.13; earlier revisions judged expiry on the receiver's clock, which made an agent's history unmergeable once its certificate lapsed) — and then verify the record under the certificate's agent key. A signer therefore attaches the certificate that covers its own signing time, and a writer holding none refuses to sign. Certificates renew: a peer MAY re-issue an agent's certificate for the same key before it lapses (the reference implementation re-issues at half-life, 24 h lifetimes by default, and keeps earlier certificates so signatures made under them stay certifiable), so an agent runs indefinitely while each signature stays judged by the certificate of its time. The accepted cost is that a stolen agent key could sign records back-dated into an old window; revocation with reason `key-compromise` (§6.1) is the remedy. Agent keys MAY persist: the reference implementation stores each subordinate agent's Ed25519 and X25519 pairs under `<agent-keystore>/<peer-id>/<local-name>/` (private keys PKCS#8 and owner-only, written atomically, loaded only after a probe proves the pair matches), and re-issues certificates at start, so an agent keeps its attested identity across restarts. A local space offers the same per-agent views without signatures, so attribution reads the same in tests and in a single process. A record that arrives with a certificate but a peer-key signature is refused, and a record without a certificate verifies under the peer key as before, so a fleet that never opts in is unchanged on the wire. Attribution is therefore per agent: a replica reports each entry as `PEER_ASSERTED`, the peer vouched for the name, or `AGENT_ATTESTED`, the agent's own key signed it. Eligibility remains an authorization decision made at the authorizer's granularity (§8 QUORUM, §11): a certificate proves who signed, never what they may do. Several agents on one node share one replica of a space through *views* (QA4 A4-7 phase 3): a view is the same space seen as another agent of the same peer — one replica, one gossip registration, one clock per node — whose writes are issued and signed by that agent, whose take claims and completions name that agent as holder, and whose admission (§7.5) is judged for that agent, so two views of one replica can be admitted differently. A view has no state of its own and cannot act as another peer's agent. The binder (§10.3) hands an agent with a key of its own views of every space it is bound to, and its AgentCard (§6.1) carries the agent's public key in an appended field, so readers see the card's agent is attested. A take claim made through a view is signed by the view's agent key: the proof still carries the holder's *peer* key (so it hashes to the holder's peer as it always has) and appends the peer's certificate, and a receiver applies the same two-key rule it applies to records — certificate under the peer key for exactly the claim's holder, covering the claim's stamp (which a renewal keeps), then the signature under the certificate's agent key; a proof carrying a certificate but a peer-key signature is refused. State transitions (§11a.4) are signed by the acting agent's own key when that agent is attested (v0.1.13): the writer for its renewals and cancellations, the claim holder for its completion, so no sibling agent and not even the peer can act in an attested agent's name. An agent whose certificate carries an encryption key MAY fetch group content keys for itself through per-agent key wrap (§11a.2a, v0.1.13), sealed to that certified key, so a sibling agent on the same peer learns nothing from it.

**4.3 DID interoperability (informative).** A PeerID is representable as `did:key:…`, and a resolver SPI allows group membership policies to accept W3C DIDs and verifiable credentials issued elsewhere. This keeps AgentSpaces alignable with ANP-style cross-organization identity without depending on it.

**4.4 Naming.** Advertisement identifiers are URIs under a common scheme: a group's advertisement carries `aspace://<groupID>`, a space's carries `aspace://<groupID>/<spaceName>`, and an entry is addressed as `aspace://<groupID>/<spaceName>#<entryID>`. GroupIDs and SpaceIDs are hashes of their founding documents, which makes them self-certifying: possession of the founding document proves the name, and nobody can publish a different policy under the same name, because a receiver that re-derives the identifier from the document it was handed detects the substitution.

The founding document of a group is defined precisely. The *founding fields* are the GroupAdvertisement's immutable fields in this order: `name`, `founder` (the issuer PeerID), `issued`, `ttl`, `membershipPolicy`, `defaultStrategy`, `gossip`, encoded as canonical CBOR. The founder signs those bytes with its Ed25519 identity key. The *founding document* is the pair (founding fields, founder signature), again as canonical CBOR, and `GroupID = multibase(sha-256(founding document))`. The advertisement's `id` is `"aspace://" + GroupID`. A SpaceID is `multibase(sha-256(canonical CBOR of the founding SpaceAdvertisement))`; a purely local, unadvertised space derives its SpaceID from its name instead.

## 5. Layer 1: Peering

**5.1 Peer groups.** A PeerGroup is the unit of scope: it defines a membership policy, a gossip domain, and a namespace for spaces and capabilities. A founder creates a group by publishing a signed GroupAdvertisement containing the group name, the GroupID (the self-certifying hash of §4.4), the membership policy, the default conflict strategy for the group's spaces, and gossip parameters (fan-out and period). The founding advertisement circulates as a signed document: the advertisement, the founder's raw public key, and the founder's signature over the founding fields. A receiver verifies it with four checks: the key hashes to the advertisement's issuer, the signature verifies over the founding fields, the GroupID re-derived from the founding document equals the advertisement's group, and the `id` URI names that group. Every group advertisement learned from the network MUST pass this check before a node joins or acts on it, and a node MUST NOT serve a newcomer a group whose advertisement it cannot verify. A locally configured advertisement with a literal GroupID is exempt, because it is configuration the network cannot swap, and a node never serves such an advertisement to newcomers. A peer that knows only a GroupID and a seed endpoint joins by asking the seed for the founding document (`GROUP_AD_WANT` and `GROUP_AD`, §9) and verifying the answer against the GroupID it already holds; a hostile seed can delay that join and can never substitute a policy.

The membership policy is `OPEN`; `INVITE`, which requires a credential signed by a founder or admin key; or `POLICY`, a pluggable validator SPI such as a DID credential check. All three are enforced at admission: a verified signature proves an advertisement's authorship and says nothing about whether its author may join, so an OPEN group admits every verified peer, an INVITE group admits only peers presenting a founder-signed credential bound to their PeerID, and a POLICY group admits only peers the group's validator accepts. Groups map naturally onto task teams, mirroring JXTA's subgroup pattern: an application spins up a group per mission, per tenant, or per conversation.

*Bootstrap groups (informative).* Earlier drafts named a universal `OPEN` WorldGroup as the root of discovery. Seed peers, rendezvous roles (§5.4), the starter's `seeds` configuration, and the multicast beacon (§10.1) supply bootstrap today, and a universal open group would add a Sybil and flood surface with no consumer. A deployment MAY define a well-known bootstrap group of its own; the core defines none.

**5.2 Membership and failure detection.** Within a group, every member periodically republishes a leased, signed self-advertisement (its PeerAdvertisement) on the reserved `peers` gossip stream, and this refresh is the liveness proof: a member's advertisement is a claim only that member can make. Membership state is leased (P2): the group drops, without ceremony, a member whose advertisement lapses and who neither gossips nor responds within its membership TTL. A SWIM-style probe cycle shortens detection: each round pings one random member, an unanswered ping triggers an indirect ping-req through k other members, and a member unanswered through the indirect stage is marked suspect. Suspicion is local state; any frame heard from the member clears it and refreshes its lease. AgentSpaces disseminates no JOIN, ALIVE, SUSPECT, or DEAD verdicts about third parties, because a verdict about another member is exactly the kind of unauthenticated claim the rest of the design refuses; members learn of a join from the joiner's own signed advertisement and of a departure from its absence. The membership view feeds a peer-sampling service exposed to upper layers (`PeerSampler.randomMembers(n)`), and this sampler is the primitive from which space anti-entropy, capability queries, and Layer 4 protocols all draw their partners.

**5.3 Gossip dissemination.** Each group runs a gossip bus with two channels. The rumor channel pushes new items (advertisement announcements, entry deltas, capability frames) on named streams to `fanout` sampled members, and each hop forwards to `fanout` further members while a per-item hop budget lasts. The hop budget is a constant, 6; a publisher MAY choose a smaller budget for an item, and a receiver clamps any larger inbound budget to the constant. The anti-entropy channel pulls a digest reconciliation from one random partner once per `period` per group on the node's own clock, with the first round immediately after joining, which guarantees convergence; rumor forwarding and membership probes run unpaced as frames arrive. Receivers deduplicate items by stream and payload hash (`sha-256` of the item payload) against a bounded seen-cache, so an attacker cannot suppress an honest item by pre-seeding its identifier. Expected propagation is O(log N) rounds. The GroupAdvertisement supplies `fanout` and `period` (defaults 3 and one second); no view-size parameter exists, because membership views are full and leased. §9 gives the envelope.

**5.4 Topology roles.** Any peer may additionally serve as a Rendezvous, which maintains a larger advertisement cache (four times the default bound in the reference implementation) and answers discovery queries for peers with constrained resources or short uptimes (edge sensors, mobile robots), or as a Relay, which forwards traffic for NAT-restricted peers. Peers advertise these roles (RendezvousAdvertisement and so on) under leases. A group with no rendezvous still functions on pure gossip and degrades only in discovery latency. JXTA gave rendezvous peers loosely consistent authoritative state; AgentSpaces rendezvous peers serve purely as caches, so losing one never affects correctness.

**5.5 Transport SPI.** The transport interface is a standards-based interface, `Transport { dial(PeerAddr), listen(), capabilities() }`. The reference implementation ships three bindings: an in-JVM loopback (powering tests and single-process fleets), TCP (length-prefixed frames over a socket), and QUIC (RFC 9000, added in v0.1.4 as `agentspaces-transport-quic`): the same length-prefixed frames over one bidirectional QUIC stream per peer connection, giving transport-level encryption always on, loss recovery, and connection migration, with everything above the transport unchanged. The QUIC handshake uses, by default, an ephemeral, per-process, unverified certificate: peer authentication lives in the signed-envelope layer against self-certifying PeerIDs (§11), identically over every transport, and QUIC's TLS supplies confidentiality alone. Constructed with the peer identity, both the QUIC binding and the TLS binding of TCP (`tls` scheme, TLS 1.3 per RFC 8446, shipped in v0.1.6) instead present the channel certificate of §5.6 and attest the remote end, which enables the per-frame signature elision that section specifies. Both bindings honour the same attestation rules (v0.1.13): both offer the enterprise-CA mode of §5.6 with a refreshable trust source, both attest a remote end from the full chain its handshake presented, and neither uses session resumption or 0-RTT early data, so every attestation rests on a full handshake that proved possession of the presented key. A WebSocket (RFC 6455) binding and bridges to Zenoh/DDS (robot fleets) or shared filesystems (air-gapped sync) plug in through the same SPI; they are planned and have not shipped. Peer reachability information travels inside PeerAdvertisements as a list of `(transport, addr, priority)` endpoints, so a peer advertises `quic` and `tcp` endpoints side by side. Dialers try endpoints in ascending `priority`, keep the advertised order among equal priorities, and skip endpoints whose scheme no configured transport serves; a listener declares the priority of each endpoint it advertises when it binds.

**5.6 Channel authentication (v0.1.6, normative).** The envelope signature of §9 authenticates one hop. When the transport itself authenticates the channel, that per-frame signature repeats work the handshake already did, at roughly a thousand times the cost of the frame's serialization, so v0.1.6 lets an authenticated channel stand in for it. The mechanism has four parts.

*Channel certificates.* A peer generates a fresh EC P-256 key for TLS and wraps it in a self-signed X.509 certificate whose X.509 signature is the peer's Ed25519 identity signature over the TBSCertificate (signature algorithm id-Ed25519, 1.3.101.112, RFC 8410), with the raw 32-byte identity key published in the subject CN (multibase base58btc). The TLS handshake (RFC 8446) proves possession of the P-256 key; the endorsement proves the identity stands behind exactly that key; together they bind the channel to `PeerID = multibase(sha-256(identity key))`. No CA chain participates: the verifier's only trust decision is this PeerID pin, the raw-public-key trust model (RFC 7250) with a certificate as carrier. The identity's private key signs once, at certificate generation, and never enters the TLS machinery.

*Attestation.* A transport that verified the remote end's channel certificate reports the bound PeerID from its connection (`attestedPeer()` in the SPI). Verification is: signature algorithm is id-Ed25519, the CN decodes to a 32-byte key, and that key's Ed25519 signature over the TBSCertificate verifies. A remote end presenting no certificate, or one failing any check, still connects; its channel is merely unattested and its frames stay fully signed. Both the `tls` and `quic` bindings attest mutually (each side presents its certificate; the server requests but does not require the client's).

*Frame relaxation.* The §9 envelope wrapper's key and signature fields become optional. A frame carrying both is a signed frame, acceptable from any connection, exactly as before. A frame carrying neither is a channel-attested frame: a receiver accepts it only when the connection's attested PeerID equals the envelope's `from`; every other receiver drops it. A frame carrying exactly one of the two fields is always dropped. Only self-originated envelopes may travel bare: the inner frame of a RELAY_FRAME remains the origin's fully signed frame verbatim, because the relay's channel attests the relay alone.

*Negotiation.* A node willing to receive channel-attested frames announces it with a signed `CHANNEL_HELLO` envelope on the attested connection, whose body carries `acceptsBare` and echoes the PeerID the sender attested for the remote end, binding the announcement to that very channel; the receiver honors it only when the echo names itself and the connection's attestation names the announcer. The announcement governs only what a sender elides. A receiver accepts a bare frame whenever the connection attests the frame's sender, whatever mode the receiver itself announced, because the acceptance rule of the previous paragraph is already sufficient: bare frames are accepted from the attested peer alone. A sender elides signatures only toward peers that announced, so fleets mix v0.1.5 and v0.1.6 nodes, and SIGNED-mode and ATTESTED-mode nodes, freely. Forcing signed mode is the only downgrade an attacker can cause, and signed mode gives up nothing but speed.

*Required attestation (v0.1.10, normative for the enterprise-CA mode).* A node MAY require attestation. In that mode every inbound frame, admission frames included, MUST arrive on a connection whose attested PeerID equals the envelope's `from`; the node logs and drops a frame on an unattested connection or on a connection attested for someone else, and it drops the inner frames of `RELAY_FRAME`, because a relay's channel attests the relay alone. The check runs after replay deduplication and never strikes the sender. A bootstrap introduction needs an attested channel, and a peer advertisement forwarded by a third party refreshes existing members and admits nobody. When a signed frame from a current member arrives on a connection that attests nothing, the connection has just completed a fresh handshake that attests nothing, which is what a CA revocation, expiry, or credential replacement looks like at this layer: the node evicts the member from its membership view, closes the cached connection, and refuses the member at dispatch until one of its frames arrives on an attested connection again. A frame attested for someone else is dropped and never evicts, so an attested party cannot evict a member by replaying its signed frames. This is how the CA's revocation becomes the authoritative eject that v0.1.9 promised; the `tls` and `quic` bindings accept a refreshable trust source so a deployment can load new CRLs without restarting the peer. A node in this mode registers attesting transports alone: a plaintext channel attests nobody, so dialing a peer's plaintext endpoint would arrive unattested at the far end and have this node evicted there. The starter enforces that rule from one property, `agentspaces.transport.tls.require-attestation`, which selects the mode, drops the plaintext binding, and refuses to start when TLS is off.

What this changes and what it leaves alone: the elided signature is hop authenticity only. The evidence layer, record signatures (§7.1), authenticated state transitions (§11a.4), signed claims, signed advertisements, and join credentials, is untouched, travels inside frame bodies, and verifies end to end regardless of how the enclosing frame was authenticated.

## 6. Layer 2: Advertisements and Discovery

**6.1 The advertisement model.** An Advertisement is a signed, typed, TTL-bearing document, shown here in its JSON (RFC 8259) rendering; on the wire it travels as canonical CBOR inside the signed wrapper of §9:

```json
{
  "type": "aspace:CapabilityAdvertisement",
  "id": "aspace://grp-7f3a…/cap/task-auction/peer-09c1…",
  "issuer": "peer:09c1…",
  "group": "grp-7f3a…",
  "issued": "2026-08-26T14:02:11Z",
  "ttl": "PT15M",
  "body": { …type-specific… },
  "sig": "ed25519:…"
}
```

The core defines these normative advertisement types: PeerAdvertisement (endpoints, roles, resource hints); GroupAdvertisement (§5.1); SpaceAdvertisement (space name, entry schema hints that describe the shape of data in the space, the conflict strategy, the admission rule, `GROUP` or `ALLOWLIST`, and the replication mode, `FULL` or `TAG_SHARDED`; §7.5); CapabilityAdvertisement (§8); AgentCard, a capability card describing an agent through a human- and LLM-readable description, the goals it can pursue, the input and output entry types it consumes and produces, cost hints, and space bindings, a map from each consumed entry type to the space the agent takes it from, which lets a caller route an input entry without configuring routes by hand (§10.6); and AssetCard (§6.1a, added in v0.1.1), the AgentCard's sibling for peers that provide data and pursue no goals. An AgentCard carries no network endpoints: agents are reached by writing entries, and the A2A gateway (§12) supplies HTTP endpoints where foreign clients need them. The AgentCard stays deliberately close in spirit to A2A Agent Cards so a gateway can translate mechanically; within AgentSpaces, the Embabel extension generates AgentCards from Embabel metadata (§10.4), and the remote-actions bridge consumes them as planner actions (§10.6). The admission and replication fields of the SpaceAdvertisement and the space bindings of the AgentCard are additive record fields appended in v0.1.10; a decoder that lacks them ignores them, and a card without bindings routes as before. The AgentCard's `agentPublicKey` (v0.1.11, QA4 A4-7 phase 3) is appended likewise and omitted when absent: present, it is the raw Ed25519 key the issuing peer certified for the card's agent (§4.2), so a reader can tell an attested agent from an asserted name; absent, the card is byte-identical to the v0.1.10 card. Its `agentCertificate` (v0.1.13), appended after `actions` and omitted when absent, carries the peer's certificate for that key, so the attestation is provable: a cache MUST refuse a card whose certificate does not certify exactly its agent and `agentPublicKey`, verify under the card issuer's key, and cover the card's `issued`. The AgentCard's `actions` (v0.1.13) is appended after `agentPublicKey` and omitted when absent: each `CardAction(name, description, consumes, produces, space, kind)` declares one bound method — its name (the method name, unique within the card), what it does, the schemas it consumes and produces (empty for a void action), the space it takes or watches its input in, and how it is bound (`take`, `notify`, `ordered-take`, `ballot`, `on-decision`, or `embabel-action`). A card declares at most 64 actions, each description at most 512 characters, and the binder sorts them by name so a card's bytes do not depend on reflection order. Readers that understand actions use the declared pairs instead of forming the cross product of the card's flat `consumes` and `produces` lists; a card without actions is byte-identical to the v0.1.12 card and reads as before.

The RevocationAdvertisement (v0.1.9) is signed and typed like the others and is deliberately excluded from the ad-cache: a revocation is a permanent verdict, so its TTL bounds re-gossip and never the withdrawal itself, and caching it beside leased advertisements would let it expire. Publishing a revocation through discovery routes it to the group's revocation registry, which verifies the signature and the signer's authority before accepting it; an accepted revocation then travels on the dedicated `revocations` gossip stream with anti-entropy so late joiners converge, and a refused revocation is never gossiped. Enforcement does not stop at the revoked peer's own frames (v0.1.13, ASF-047): a member never admits the revoked PeerID to its membership view from anyone's copy of its self-advertisement, and never forwards that advertisement; discovery purges and thereafter refuses the revoked peer's advertisements; and a space refuses any state or claim whose *actor* is revoked — the record's issuer for a write, renewal, or cancellation, the claim holder for a claim or a completion — for entries the replica does not already hold. Replicas that already hold the revoked peer's entries keep them (§7.5: revocation does not remove work already admitted), an honest holder's completion of such an entry still lands, and a refusal is acknowledged in the space digest as an `a:<line>` entry so anti-entropy partners stop re-offering it. A late joiner therefore never adopts a revoked peer's history; that is the intended cost of revocation.

*One record per revoked peer (v0.1.13, normative).* A registry keeps exactly one record per revoked PeerID, chosen by a total order every member computes alike, so registries converge: the issuer's authority first (the founder, then a revocation rooted in the CA with evidence, then any other issuer a deployment's validator admits), then the newer `issued`, then the larger SHA-256 of the signed bytes. A lower-ranked statement never replaces a higher-ranked one whatever its stamp, so a founder's rotation cannot be erased by a later record from anyone else, and only the founder may name a `successor`. Acceptance side effects fire once per peer, on its first record.

*CA-rooted peer revocation (v0.1.13, normative for the enterprise-CA mode).* A RevocationAdvertisement may append `evidence`, the CBOR `RevocationEvidence(chain, crl?)`: the revoked peer's channel chain as DER, leaf first, at most four certificates, and optionally the CRL revoking its leaf as DER, at most 64 KiB, with at most 96 KiB in all; absent evidence is omitted, so earlier revocations keep their bytes. Under the `founder-or-ca` validator a member accepts a revocation from any issuer when its evidence proves, judged at the revocation's own `issued` instant so every member reaches the same verdict, that the chain validates to the member's CA anchors, that the leaf's CN binds the revoked PeerID, and that a CRL signed by the leaf's issuer (the evidence's, or one the member holds) lists the leaf for a reason that withdraws the identity: `keyCompromise`, `cACompromise`, `privilegeWithdrawn`, `cessationOfOperation`, or `unspecified`. A `superseded`, `affiliationChanged`, or `certificateHold` entry, an unverifiable or stale CRL, and a revocation naming a successor never authorize one; those remain connection-level, where attestation already refuses the leaf. No network is consulted on this path. A node in that mode judges every presented chain when a connection attaches and again whenever its trust changes (a CRL refresh); a leaf revoked for an authorizing reason makes it cut the link and, at most once per peer, root the revocation itself with the chain and CRL as evidence. The founder-rooted default is unchanged and ignores evidence.

*Revoking what peers certify (v0.1.13, normative).* A `CredentialRevocation` advertisement revokes something certified other than a peer identity: an `AGENT` (every key of an AgentID), an `AGENT_KEY` (one agent key, by the SHA-256 of its raw public key), an `X509_LEAF` (one channel certificate, by the SHA-256 of its DER), or a `JOIN_CREDENTIAL` (by the SHA-256 of its bytes). Its fields are `id = aspace://<group>/revocation/<kind>/<canonical target>`, `issuer`, `group`, `issued`, `ttl`, `target`, `reason` (one of `key-compromise`, `retired`, `superseded`, `privilege-withdrawn`, `unspecified`), an optional `effectiveFrom` no later than `issued`, and optional `evidence`. The founder may revoke any target, and an agent's own peer may revoke that agent and its keys; nobody else, unless a deployment's validator ranks them. It travels like a peer revocation, routed by discovery to the group's credential-revocation registry and gossiped on the `credential-revocations` stream with anti-entropy; old nodes ignore the stream. The registry keeps one record per target under the same total order (founder, trust root, the target's own peer, other), and holds it for the life of the process. Enforcement follows a freeze rule. What a replica already holds stays. A newly arriving signature by a revoked agent or key is refused when the reason is `key-compromise`, whatever its claimed signing time, because a stolen key can back-date; under any other reason it is refused only when signed at or after `effectiveFrom` (else `issued`), so a late joiner still receives the target's history. Records are judged at their issue stamp and agent-signed states at their `signedAt`; claims are refused under any revocation, because a renewal keeps the claim's original stamp, so a revoked holder's take lapses and the entry returns to the space, and its completion is refused. Discovery refuses and purges a revoked agent's cards; key-wrap holders serve it no key (§11a.2a); a vote tally excludes the ballots of a revoked agent (or of an agent whose peer is revoked) at every recount, so a decision may reopen (§8), while an `AGENT_KEY` revocation does not reach ballots already merged, since a tally does not see which key signed a ballot, and stops the key's new ballots where the space refuses them at merge; each group's authorizer refuses it (§11); a node refuses to write or take as it locally and unbinds it. A revoked join credential admits nobody, and a member it admitted is evicted. A revoked X.509 leaf cuts every link presenting it, now and on every later handshake.

**6.1a The AssetCard (v0.1.1 addendum).** A peer needs no agents to participate in a group (P4, P6), and the AssetCard is the advertisement type that makes such peers useful: a connector process joins a group on behalf of a warehouse, database, search cluster, message broker, or web service and publishes one signed, TTL-bearing AssetCard per asset it holds. An AssetCard carries the asset name and source URI, a human- and LLM-readable description, the declared shape of data (an entry schema name, table structure, index mapping, or message format), the freshness or update cadence, size and cost hints, and an access binding naming the mode and the capability that serves queries. Agents discover assets exactly as they discover agents: structurally through the ad-cache (`find(AssetCard.class, predicate)`) or by meaning through the semantic-discovery capability, and the connector normalizes incongruent data from its source into typed entries whose shape the card declares.

Connectors serve data in two styles, and most implement both. A catalog provider answers on demand through a data-query capability (`aspace:cap/data-query`, defined outside the core per P6): agents write typed query entries into an agreed space and the connector writes result entries back, inline when they fit the 64 KiB inline limit and content-addressed through the group's block exchange when they are bulk, so retries, observability, and service choreography across connectors come free with the space. The connector runtime builds its data space with a block exchange attached, and the query client reads bulk results through the same exchange, so a large catalog result travels as a block and the space replicates only its record. A materializing provider pushes changes into the space as its source changes: the provider implements `materialize(sink)` and hands the runtime one `Change(asset, key, row)` per changed row, and the runtime writes each as an `AssetChange(asset, key, row, servedBy)` entry leased for the asset's declared freshness, so the lease is the retention policy and a row the source stops refreshing ages out. Subscribing agents register `notify(Template.of(AssetChange.class), …)` and receive complex event processing over enterprise events. In both styles the lease is the liveness contract (P2): a connector that goes down stops refreshing, and its assets age out of every cache with no failure-detection machinery.

**6.1b Unknown values (v0.1.11, normative).** Enumerated advertisement fields (membership policy, conflict strategy, admission, replication) are encoded as their names. A receiver that meets a name it does not know MUST treat the advertisement as undecodable and drop it without caching or forwarding it, exactly as it treats a malformed one; it MUST NOT substitute a default. This is what lets a revision add a value (v0.1.11 added two admission rules) without a wire-format change: an older peer refuses the advertisement it cannot interpret, and the space or group it describes stays invisible to that peer rather than being joined under the wrong rule.

**6.2 TTL semantics.** `ttl` bounds cache lifetime from `issued`; caches MUST evict expired advertisements and MUST NOT forward them. Issuers refresh by re-publishing with a new `issued`. Recommended defaults: PeerAdvertisement 10 minutes; AgentCard 15 minutes; SpaceAdvertisement 15 minutes; GroupAdvertisement 24 hours; capability advertisements 15 minutes. Short TTLs serve as the liveness mechanism (P2): the freshness of an advertisement answers the question "does this peer still offer this capability?" with no failure-detection round trips.

**6.3 The ad-cache and discovery.** Every peer maintains a local advertisement cache per group, which gossip populates. Discovery is therefore local-first: `discovery.find(type, predicate)` queries the local cache, and `remoteFind` escalates in parallel to the group's rendezvous peers (a direct query to each) and to a scoped gossip query (a rumor carrying the predicate, with answers routed back to the asker) under the hop budget, then waits for answers and re-runs the local scan. `remoteFind` validates the predicate against the advertisement type before it publishes anything, so a predicate naming an unknown field fails immediately with an argument error and never reaches the network. Core predicates are structural (type plus field matchers). Layer 4 defines a semantic-discovery capability service (embedding-based matching over AgentCard descriptions) so that the base protocol stays model-free.

## 7. Layer 3: The AgentSpace (Distributed Tuple Space)

### 7.1 Data model

An Entry is any serializable domain object (Java record or POJO) registered with the space's schema registry. The space wraps each written entry in an EntryRecord:

```
EntryRecord {
  entryId     : UUID (issuer-generated)
  spaceId     : URI
  type        : schema name + version
  payload     : canonical serialized entry  (inline if ≤ 64 KiB)
  payloadRef  : content hash (CID)          (for large payloads; fetched via swarming)
  issuer      : AgentID
  issued      : timestamp (hybrid logical clock)
  lease       : { holder, expires, kind: WRITE | TAKE }
  tags        : map<string,string>          (indexable)
  sig         : issuer signature
  keyEpoch    : content-key epoch           (v0.1.13, appended; omitted for epoch 0, §11a.1)
}
```

*Reserved types (v0.1.14).* A few entry types are the library's own and every replica MUST name them identically whatever registry an application uses for its domain types: `SpaceCredential` (§7.5), the vote's `Proposal`, `Ballot`, and `Decision` (§8), and `JoinTicket` (§10.3), the ticket a fan-in writes and takes in its fleet-wide modes. A registry that names an application's types by an ontology namespace delegates everything else to the default `<fully.qualified.ClassName>#v1` rule. A client that knows no joins stores and replicates a `JoinTicket` as any foreign type and never acts on it.

A replica gossips each record inside its replication state, which carries the mutable CRDT fields and their signature (§7.3, §11a.4):

```
EntryStateDto {
  record           : EntryRecord
  issuerPublicKey  : the issuer peer's raw Ed25519 key
  adds, removes    : observed-remove dots
  leaseStamp       : HLC stamp of the lease register
  leaseValue       : the current lease
  completed        : monotone completion flag
  stateSig         : signature over the mutable state (§11a.4)
  agentCertificate : the issuer agent's certificate     (v0.1.11, appended; omitted when absent, §4.2)
  stateCertificate : the state signer's certificate     (v0.1.13, appended; omitted when absent)
  signer           : AgentID of the state signer        (v0.1.13, appended; omitted when absent)
  signedAt         : HLC stamp of the state signature   (v0.1.13, appended; omitted when absent)
}
```

Large payloads (model weights, images, sensor batches) are content-addressed, and a Bitswap-style block exchange transfers them out of band; the space replicates only the record. This keeps gossip traffic small and provides deduplication and integrity checking at no extra cost.

### 7.2 Operations

The space exposes the Linda quartet plus events, all lease-aware.

`write(entry, lease)` publishes an entry and returns an `EntryHandle` for renewal or cancellation. The write lease is the entry's TTL, so unrenewed entries vanish (P2).

`read(template, timeout)` and `readAll(template, limit)` perform non-destructive matches.

*Matching order (v0.1.7).* Within one entry type, a single-node space evaluates candidates in insertion order, so `read` and `take` prefer the oldest matching entry of the template's type: first-in first-out among equals. The implementation partitions storage by entry class (matching never scans entries a template's type cannot accept), and the ordering guarantee is the observable face of that index. Across different entry types, and on replicated spaces, where arrival order differs per replica and take arbitration (§7.4) decides winners, no inter-entry order is promised.

`take(template, takeLease, timeout)` performs a destructive, exclusive match under the space's conflict strategy (§7.4). The taker holds a TAKE lease; if the taker dies or lets the lease lapse without calling `complete()`, the entry reappears. This reproduces the JavaSpaces crash-recovery idiom. JavaSpaces built it on transactions; AgentSpaces builds it on leases.

`complete(handle, resultEntry?)` consumes the taken entry permanently and optionally writes a result atomically: the result record is built and validated before the completion commits, so a result that cannot be written never leaves the task completed-but-resultless.

`notify(template, listener, lease)` registers a leased subscription. Events arrive at least once, with entry-record deduplication, and give applications a foundation for complex event processing (CEP) over the entry stream. The event kinds are WRITTEN, TAKEN, COMPLETED, EXPIRED, and REAPPEARED; a replicated space emits EXPIRED and REAPPEARED from its lease sweep exactly once per transition, so a subscriber can build a watchdog on a replicated space as on a local one.

Templates are typed: `Template.of(TaskEntry.class).where("kind", eq("summarize")).where("priority", gte(3))`. Matching runs against local replicas, so reads never block on the network (local-first, P7). `read(template, ConsistencyHint.FRESH, timeout)` and `readAll(template, ConsistencyHint.FRESH, limit)` perform exactly one anti-entropy pull toward one random group member and then match locally; `ConsistencyHint.LOCAL` is the default and matches the replica as it stands. Content-addressed entries (§7.1) follow the same rule: a read never waits on a block. A read that meets a record whose block is absent starts one background fetch; a blocking `read` awaits that fetch within its own timeout, and `readAll` skips entries whose block has not arrived.

*Tag conditions (v0.1.14).* A template MAY carry conditions on the record's tags beside its field predicates: `Template.of(Finding.class).whereTag("rdf:type", eq(iri))`, or `hasTag("region")`. A replica judges tag conditions against `EntryRecord.tags` after the type comparison and before it decodes the payload, so the matching order within a type is type, tags, claim, decode, fields, and a keyless relay or a tag-sharded replica can select by tag without holding the content key, which is why `type` and `tags` stay plaintext under group content keys (§11). Tag conditions apply to every verb, including the subscription a `notify` registers. A read MAY return the record's metadata beside the decoded value (`readAllEntries`: entry id, authenticated issuer, attestation, tags, lease, issue stamp), and a subscription event carries the same view, so a listener reads an entry's tags without a second read. A completion MAY write its result with tags, atomically with the completion, as a write can.

### 7.3 Replication

A space replicates across the peers of its group (full replication by default; tag-sharded partial replication is also implemented, so a replica can hold only the slice of a space its tag predicate selects). The replica state is a delta-CRDT: the entry set is an OR-Set keyed by `entryId` with add and remove semantics, the lease fields are last-writer-wins registers ordered by hybrid logical clock, and tombstones persist for twice the maximum lease before garbage collection. The collection rule is exact: a replica collects an entry that is completed, withdrawn, or past its write lease once `leaseExpiry + 2 × maxLease` has passed, where `maxLease` is the largest lease the replica has observed, capped at 24 hours, or a value the builder fixes. A replica MUST refuse a state re-offered by a lagging partner once that state is past the collection horizon, so a collected tombstone never returns. Deltas ride the group's rumor channel, and digests reconcile via anti-entropy. Consequently, replicas that have seen the same updates are identical, partitions merge deterministically, and a disconnected robot's local space continues to serve reads, writes, and (strategy permitting) takes. The mutable CRDT fields a delta carries are themselves authenticated (§11a.4), so a relay or any other member cannot forge a completion, a removal, or a lease that would make a signed entry vanish.

### 7.4 Conflict-resolution strategies (`take` exclusivity)

Exclusive take is the one operation CRDTs cannot express, so it is where strategies differ. A space declares its strategy in its SpaceAdvertisement. The API stays identical across strategies, which makes the strategy choice orthogonal to application code; only the guarantees and the latency change. The core defines three strategies.

`LEASE_RACE` (default). Takers write a claim (a TAKE-lease LWW register on the entry) and wait one gossip settle-window (configurable, default twice the gossip period). Arbitration is deterministic: the claim with the lowest `(hlc, peerId)` pair takes the entry, and the other takers receive an empty result and retry elsewhere. The strategy guarantees at most one completed take per entry, because the CRDT merge rule rejects a second `complete`; transient duplicate work remains possible during partitions. The cost is one settle-window of latency and zero extra machinery. This default suits idempotent tasks, and agent workloads should strive to be idempotent.

`AUCTION` (probabilistic, cost-aware). The take is a bid: `take(template, bid(cost))`. Rounds of a CBAA/CBBA-style auction-plus-consensus among the current bidders allocate the entries matching the template: each peer locally selects the tasks it expects to secure at its bid, gossips its winner list, and conflicts resolve in favor of the better bid with deterministic tie-breaks, converging in O(diameter) gossip rounds to a conflict-free assignment. This turns the space itself into a decentralized least-cost task allocator: agents write tasks, agents bid, and the space converges. The application supplies the bid functions, which may be learned policies.

`ORDERED` (strict). Takes serialize through an ordered-log capability service (a small Raft quorum elected within the group; see §8). This yields exactly-once take semantics at the price of quorum liveness requirements, and it suits only non-idempotent, high-stakes entries (P1). ORDERED is not selected as a space strategy; instead the ordered-log capability's take coordinator drives the takes over the log, and its accept decision is a pure function of committed state (the claim's generation), so every member arbitrates identically regardless of when it applies a log entry. Arbitration under ORDERED is by epoch alone: the log admits exactly one claim per epoch (a committed claim's epoch MUST equal the entry's current epoch plus one), so no two committed claims ever share an epoch and the lattice's stamp comparison is never reached for them. A log's decision is authoritative for its epoch: a replica that installed a committed claim MUST NOT let a gossip-borne claim at the same or a lower epoch displace it, while an identical claim may still merge (so a replica holding an unattested copy is upgraded by the attested one). The committed claim is installed at every replica exactly as its holder signed it, with the holder's key and signature carried through the log unchanged; the coordinator MUST NOT re-sign or re-stamp it, because a completion is authenticated against the key the stored claim carries (§11a.4), and a claim signed by any other key can authenticate no completion at any other replica. (v0.1.11, QA4 A4-5: the reference implementation had re-stamped committed claims with the log index and re-signed them locally, which bought arbitration priority at the cost of the holder's attestation; a replica that missed the completion rumor could then never converge by anti-entropy, whose digest keys claims by content.)

*Claim stamps (v0.1.10).* Because LEASE_RACE prefers the lowest stamp, a replica bounds the stamps it will merge, so an admitted member cannot win every race by back-dating its claims. Two rules apply. A claim's stamp MUST NOT precede the entry's `issued` stamp by more than the clock drift ceiling (ten minutes, §11), which is a pure function of the two stamps and so decides identically on every replica. A claim that contests another holder's live claim MUST NOT be older than the replica's current time minus the drift ceiling minus one settle window. A replica drops a claim that breaks either rule and strikes its signer once per distinct claim, and it feeds the stamps of the claims and leases it accepts into its own hybrid logical clock under the bounded-drift regime.

*What the two rules leave open (recorded 2026-09-23, gate2-review G2-2).* The second rule judges a claim against the claim a replica has already merged, so its outcome depends on what that replica saw first. Three consequences follow, and a deployment should know all three. A claim on an entry no replica has claimed yet is judged by the first rule alone, so an admitted member may stamp its claim at the entry's `issued` moment and beat every later honest claimant at any replica its claim reaches first: broad TAKE scope therefore carries the power to win contested races, and an operator who wants takers to compete on equal terms grants that scope narrowly. Two replicas that merged different first claims keep different winners, and anti-entropy does not reconcile them, because each replica applies the second rule again to the other's far-past claim and refuses it. A legitimately old claim from a healed partition meets the same rule: replicas that merged a newer claim first refuse it and strike its signer, while the returning replica keeps its own claim as the winner, so an honest peer can accrue strikes for a claim it made before the partition. A fleet that needs strict fairness or strict convergence here should narrow TAKE scope rather than rely on the arbitration alone.

A space fixes one strategy for all of its entries, which keeps the semantics legible. An application that needs several semantics uses several spaces (`tasks-bulk` on LEASE_RACE, `tasks-auction` on AUCTION, `commits` on ORDERED), the way it would use multiple queues.

### 7.5 Space lifecycle

A member creates a space by publishing a SpaceAdvertisement into a group, and any group member may then attach. The advertisement's `id` is `aspace://<groupID>/<spaceName>`, and it carries the space name, the conflict strategy, schema hints naming the entry types the space expects, the admission rule, and the replication mode (§6.1). A replicated space built with a discovery publisher publishes its advertisement on creation, and the founder keeps it alive by refreshing it within its TTL (15 minutes by default); any member finds the space by name with `find(SpaceAdvertisement.class, …)`, and an advertisement nobody refreshes lapses from every cache like any other. Every space the Spring starter configures publishes its advertisement this way and shares its group's block exchange (§10.1).

Space admission decides who may write, take, and complete, and the advertisement carries the rule so every member applies the same one. Under `GROUP`, the default, every group member may. Under `ALLOWLIST`, only the AgentIDs the founder lists may: a local `write`, `take`, or `complete` by an unlisted agent fails with a `SpaceAdmissionException`, an inbound record or claim from an unlisted agent is dropped and reported as misbehaviour, and reads stay open to the whole group. The starter configures the rule per space with `admission` and `allowed-agents` (§10.1) and fails fast on an empty allowlist, since a space nobody may write to is a configuration error.

Under `CREDENTIAL` (v0.1.11), the credential issuer, one PeerID named when the space is built, admits agents by writing credentials into the space itself. A space credential is an ordinary entry of the reserved type `SpaceCredential#v1` naming an AgentID and its scopes (`WRITE`, `TAKE`, or both), written under a lease equal to the credential's validity. The record signature of §7.1 already binds the entry to its issuer, every replica already stores and replicates it, and its lease is its expiry, so the mechanism adds no signature scheme and nothing on the wire. A replica MUST drop a `SpaceCredential` record whose issuer is any peer other than the configured credential issuer and MUST report it as misbehaviour. The credential issuer itself is always admitted to write and take. Any other agent is admitted for a scope when a live credential naming that AgentID and scope is present in the local replica, and replicas judge each transition once, as it arrives: a new entry needs its issuer's `WRITE`, a completion needs the claim holder's `TAKE`, a lease renewal needs the issuer's `WRITE`, and a withdrawal needs nothing. A credential that later lapses or is revoked therefore does not remove work it already admitted. A group-level revocation of an agent, an agent key, or a peer, as distinct from cancelling a space credential, follows the freeze rule stated in the `CredentialRevocation` paragraph of §6.1: what a replica already holds stays, and newly arriving signatures are refused by reason and signing time. A record or claim from an agent with no live local credential is dropped without a strike, since the credential may simply not have arrived yet; anti-entropy re-offers the delta on the next round and it merges once the credential is present. Under `ALLOWLIST` a refusal still strikes, because the list is local configuration and cannot be late. Admission is judged for the *acting agent*, never for the peer: when several agents of one peer share a replica through views (§4.2, QA4 A4-7 phase 3), each view's writes, claims, and completions are admitted or refused on that agent's own standing under `ALLOWLIST` and `CREDENTIAL`, so one agent of a peer may be admitted while its sibling is refused. `grant(agent, scopes, validity)` writes the credential, and only the issuer's node may call it; `revoke(agent)` cancels the agent's live credentials, and the cancellation replicates like any removal, so a revocation reaches the fleet within one gossip round. A credential the issuer stops renewing lapses on its own, which is P2 applied to admission. Credential entries replicate to every shard of a tag-sharded space regardless of the shard predicate, and the credential type stays out of the advertisement's schema hints.

Under `AUTHORIZER` (v0.1.11), an agent may write when the space's `Authorizer` (§11) permits `SPACE_WRITE` for its peer in the scope of the space name, and may take or complete when it permits `SPACE_TAKE`. A space thereby defers admission to the security profile's answerer: the group's membership grants under `dev-local` and `mtls`, the identity provider's scopes (`aspace:space-write:<space>`, `aspace:space-take:<space>`, or the unscoped forms for every space) under `mtls-oidc` and `zero-trust`. An `AUTHORIZER` refusal drops without a strike, like a `CREDENTIAL` refusal, because a token can arrive late exactly as a credential can. The starter configures either rule with `admission: credential` (with `credential-issuer`, defaulting to this node) or `admission: authorizer` (§10.1).

The founders lease the space itself. A space built with a founder lease turns read-only when that lease lapses, and a founder may also mark it read-only explicitly or renew the founder lease while it still runs. A read-only space refuses `write` and `take` with a `SpaceReadOnlyException`, still serves reads and subscriptions, and still accepts a bare `complete` so a take already in flight can finish; it stops refreshing its advertisement, so the advertisement's lapse is how the fleet learns the space has been released. Garbage collection of a read-only space after its longest entry lease is planned; today its tombstones are collected under the §7.3 rule and the space object stays attached until its host closes it.

## 8. Layer 4: Capability Services

A capability service is any protocol beyond the core that a peer offers to its group. The pattern is uniform. First, the provider publishes a CapabilityAdvertisement carrying the capability type URI, version, endpoint binding (a space it watches, or a direct pipe), parameters, cost hints, and TTL. Second, consumers discover it through the ad-cache. Third, binding is either space-mediated (requests and responses flow as entries in an agreed space, giving retries, observability, and decoupling at no extra cost; the right default for request/response capabilities, and what `vote` uses) or direct (a bidirectional pipe, the right choice for continuous protocols whose exchange rate would otherwise fill a space with entries nobody reads, and what `aggregate`, `gossip-learn`, `ordered-log`, `semantic-discovery`, and `key-wrap` use). The trade is explicit: a pipe-bound capability gives up the space's retries, its audit trail, and its visibility to the fleet console, and takes on the obligation to be driven by the protocol clock described below. A pipe-bound capability that wants any of those properties back publishes them into a space deliberately, as `gossip-learn` does with its per-epoch `Evaluation` entries. Fourth, the capability's own mini-spec defines the interaction itself. Space-mediated binding also supports service choreography: an application can sequence several capability invocations through shared spaces, with each service consuming the entries the previous one produced. The capability runtime that hosts providers validates a provider's advertisement before it stores or starts the provider, and it refreshes every hosted advertisement on each refresh tick; a host drives that tick itself or asks the runtime to schedule it at a period shorter than the advertisement TTL, and the Spring starter schedules it with the card refresh (§10.4). A capability runtime drives two distinct clocks and MUST NOT conflate them. The *advertisement clock* is the refresh tick just described, measured in minutes. The *protocol clock* is `CapabilityProvider.tick()`, which advances a continuous capability's own algorithm by one round: a push-sum exchange, a gossip-learning offer, a Raft election-timer step. The runtime registers its protocol tick with the peer's tick when it is constructed, so a provider is driven because it is registered, exactly as a replicated space converges because it is constructed and joins the same clock (§7.3); an application supplies no scheduler of its own. A provider declares whether it needs the protocol clock through `requiresTick()`, and a provider whose semantics are counted in ticks rather than wall time is told the true cadence through `driverCadence(Duration)` before its first advertisement, so a tick-derived wall-clock value it advertises — the ordered log's leader lease of §8 below — describes the clock it is really driven at rather than one assumed at construction. A host that needs determinism, a test above all, detaches from the peer tick and drives the protocol tick itself, declaring its own cadence. A continuous capability that has never completed a round holds nothing but its own local contribution, and it MUST refuse to answer a fleet-wide query in that state rather than return that contribution as if it were a fleet figure.

The release standardizes the following capabilities in informative annexes; they sit outside the core normative surface.

**`aspace:cap/aggregate`** provides gossip aggregation with six operators, `sum|avg|count|min|max|quantile`, over locally reported values or over a numeric field of entries matching a template. Any member starts an epoch under an agreed identifier, and all participants converge in O(log N) rounds. One pipe frame carries the whole family as a union: a push-sum `Share` (the sender halves its mass and ships half), an `Extremum` for max-gossip, a bucketed `Histogram` vector pushed like a share, and a `Roster`, the union of 8-byte participant tokens (the first 8 bytes of `sha-256(PeerID)`). `AVG` is the push-sum ratio of value to weight; `SUM` is that mean times the roster size; `COUNT` is the roster size; `MIN` and `MAX` are the extremum; `QUANTILE` reads a cumulative histogram over a declared range (100 equal buckets by default) and is exact to within one bucket. In template mode each participant reads up to 10,000 local matches: `SUM` and `COUNT` contribute the local total and local match count, `AVG` runs a parallel epoch for the count so the result is entry-weighted, `MIN` and `MAX` contribute the local extremum (and nothing when nothing matches), and `QUANTILE` contributes one unit per match. Over a fully replicated space, template-mode `SUM` and `COUNT` count each entry once per replica, while the other operators are replication-invariant. The advertisement carries `modes=sum,avg,count,min,max,quantile`. This is the primitive for quorum sensing: fleet-wide battery, backlog depth, coverage. *Settling (v0.1.14, ISSUE-OnEstimate).* The aggregate MAY let a listener wait for an epoch to settle: `onEstimate(epochs, Settle(ticks, tolerance), listener)` is evaluated on every protocol tick after the exchange step, for every epoch the capability knows (started locally or first seen in a received share), and fires once per epoch per registration when the estimate has been present and within the tolerance of the previous tick's value (relative, absolute below 1e-9 so a zero epoch can settle) for `ticks` consecutive ticks; `Settle.FIRST` fires on the first present estimate. The listener runs off the tick thread. A settle rule does not detect a poisoned epoch; value poisoning stays an accepted risk (§11). `awaitSettled(epochId, settle, timeout)` is the blocking form; `awaitEstimate` remains the first-presence form.

**`aspace:cap/vote`** supports collective decisions in two implemented modes, `MAJORITY_GOSSIP` (preference vectors aggregated via push-sum; cheap and approximate) and `QUORUM` (explicit ballots written as entries and tallied once a configurable quorum of the electorate has voted); a third bio-inspired `CROSS_INHIBITION` mode (option competition for best-of-N choices under noise) is planned. A deployment MAY narrow the electorate further with the `Authorizer` of §11 (v0.1.12): a tally counts a ballot only when its authenticated issuer is in the electorate and `permits(issuer, VOTE, voteSpace)` holds, which lets an identity provider name the voters of a QUORUM decision with an `aspace:vote` scope as it names Raft voters and directive issuers. Both tests are evaluated locally at every tally, so a decision stays recomputable by any member that shares the replica's view of the electorate and its grants. A third test joins them in v0.1.13: a ballot whose issuer the group has revoked, or whose peer is revoked (§6.1), stops counting at the next tally, so a decision can reopen; an `@OnDecision` that already fired for the proposal does not fire again. *Counting granularity (v0.1.11, QA4 A4-7 phase 2, normative).* A tally counts identities at the granularity its authorizer speaks (§11): under `PEER` the counted identity is the ballot issuer's PeerID, so one member is one counted ballot however many agent names it writes; under `AGENT` each permitted agent counts once, and a ballot counts only when its record is `AGENT_ATTESTED` (§4.2), because a peer-asserted name is exactly what agent-level counting exists to distrust. Counting and authorization therefore never disagree, which is the invariant one-member-one-vote rests on; an implementation MUST NOT count per AgentID while authorizing per PeerID. A decision records the granularity that produced it. The electorate predicate is a liveness filter over who is currently present and is not an eligibility check: a fresh AgentCard alone admits no voter the authorizer does not permit. Proposing is an ordinary space write and is governed by the space's own admission rule rather than by `VOTE`. The electorate is a predicate over the authenticated ballot issuer, applied at every tally: the shipped rule counts a ballot while the issuer's AgentCard is unexpired in the group's ad-cache, so quorum is relative to the live electorate and a decision can reopen when cards lapse; the default electorate counts every authenticated issuer, for groups that publish no cards. A ballot for an option the proposal does not offer never counts. Ballots are signed entries, so any member can audit the tally from the space.

**Cost-aware task allocation** ships today as the `AUCTION` space strategy (§7.4): the CBAA/CBBA engine runs inside the space, so agents write tasks, bid through `take(template, bid)`, and the space converges on a least-cost assignment. A standalone `aspace:cap/task-auction` capability that exposes the same engine directly, for bundle allocation with marginal-score bidding and heterogeneous constraints outside a single space, is planned; until then the strategy is the supported surface.

**`aspace:cap/ordered-log`** elects a Raft group among volunteering members and exposes an append log whose committed commands flow to listeners in commit order, backing the ORDERED strategy through a take coordinator, which carries each submitter's signed claim through the log unchanged and installs it on every member's replica with that attestation intact (§7.4). A log instance has a fixed member set; reconfiguring means starting a new log, and single-server membership changes are future work. The leader lease is an advertisement: each member's CapabilityAdvertisement carries `members`, its `role` (`leader`, `follower`, or `candidate`), and its `term`, and the leader's alone carries `leaseUntil`, the issue instant plus its election timeout in wall time (the election timeout in ticks times the tick period, one second by default). Consumers find the leader by filtering the providers of the type for `role=leader`; a stale advertisement is superseded by a later issue or lapses with its TTL.

**`aspace:cap/gossip-learn`** provides decentralized model improvement over a mergeable-model SPI: `MergeableModel<M>` supplies `merge` (which MUST be commutative), `encode`, `decode`, and a `name`, with element-wise weight averaging over big-endian doubles as the default. Models are content-addressed: the content identifier is `base58btc(sha-256(encode(model)))`, an encoding up to 64 KiB travels inline, and a larger one is stored in the group's block exchange and travels as its CID. The exchange is a token-passing offer and accept over pipes: a node sends `OFFER` to one sampled member and locks the offered model until the reply arrives or three ticks pass; an unlocked receiver merges the offer and answers `ACCEPT` with its own pre-merge snapshot, a locked receiver or non-participant answers `BUSY`, and the offerer merges the acceptance only when the token matches its outstanding offer. Both sides therefore merge exactly the same pair, so any interleaving of concurrent exchanges conserves the fleet sum of a commutative merge, and only a lost `ACCEPT` moves mass one-sidedly, after which the offerer times out. A merge that throws abandons the exchange. The epoch protocol writes one `Evaluation(modelId, epoch, loss, contentId)` entry per local model into a metrics space under a lease when a participant ends an epoch, so per-epoch losses are auditable from the space. The advertisement carries `merge=<name>`, `exchange=offer-accept`, and `content=inline` or `content=inline,cid`. The capability serves both classic gossip learning and DFL-style neighborhood averaging, and it explicitly suits training the AUCTION bid policies in situ.

**`aspace:cap/semantic-discovery`** maintains an embedding index over the group's advertisements (AgentCards, AssetCards, and CapabilityAdvertisements) for fuzzy lookup by meaning ("who can summarize legal PDFs cheaply?", "who holds customer order history?"). The embedder sits behind an SPI so deployments choose their model; the reference implementation ships a deterministic feature-hashing embedder that works with no model at all, and richer embedders plug in without protocol changes. Peers that rank with different embeddings return different matches, so the capability advertisement names its embedder (v0.1.12): the parameters `embedder` (a stable identity such as `hashing` or `spring-ai:text-embedding-3-small`), `dimensions`, and `normalized`. A remote query SHOULD go only to peers whose advertised embedder and dimensions equal the querier's; a peer that advertises no embedder is taken to match, and a deployment may lift the requirement. Indexing entry tags directly, so agents can search live entries by meaning and not only the peers that produce them, is planned.

Third parties define capabilities by minting a type URI and publishing the mini-spec; the core enumerates none of them (P6). This is the extension point for future conversational agreement protocols, for example an argumentation and negotiation capability in which proposal and counter-proposal entries flow through a space until a vote capability closes them.

## 9. Wire Protocol Summary (normative sketch)

All messages are length-prefixed CBOR (RFC 8949) frames. A frame is a signed wrapper `{ envelope, senderPublicKey?, signature? }` around an envelope `{ ver, group, kind, from, to?, stamp, body }`: `ver` is the wire version, `group` the GroupID the frame belongs to, `kind` one of the kinds below, `from` the sender's PeerID, `to` the intended recipient's PeerID or absent on a genuinely unaddressed frame (the bootstrap self-introduction to a seed whose PeerID the sender does not yet know), `stamp` the sender's hybrid logical clock stamp, and `body` the kind-specific CBOR. The stamp fills the role earlier drafts gave an `epoch` field, and `to` binds an addressed frame to its recipient, so a receiver drops a frame whose `to` is present and names another peer. The wire version is 3 (v0.1.15): every map inside a signed or hashed structure is encoded with its entries in RFC 8949 §4.2.1 order, the shorter UTF-8 key first and bytewise between equal lengths, so the canonical bytes of a structure depend on its content and not on the order its author built a map in; record components keep their declared order. A receiver applies the per-stream deltas of one `PULL_RESP` in the order it registered the streams, never in the order the wire carries them, so a revocation registry registered before a space judges the records the same pull brings. A receiver MUST drop a frame whose `ver` is anything else before verifying its signature; the drop is counted and never strikes the sender, so a future version can coexist with this one at the cost of mutual silence.

The kinds are `PING`, `PING_REQ`, `ACK` (membership probes); `RUMOR` (one item on a named stream: an advertisement, an entry delta, a capability frame, or a discovery query); `DIGEST` and `PULL_RESP` (anti-entropy: per-stream digests and the deltas the partner lacked); `QUERY` and `QUERY_HIT` (scoped, hop-budgeted discovery, with hits routed directly to the asker); `BLOCK_WANT` and `BLOCK` (content-addressed payload exchange); `PIPE_DATA` (direct capability frames, in datagram form, multiplexed by capability type URI); `RELAY_FRAME` (a complete signed frame a RELAY-role peer forwards verbatim to a peer nobody can dial, verified end to end against the origin); `CHANNEL_HELLO` (the per-connection channel-authentication announcement of §5.6, added in v0.1.6); and, added in v0.1.10, `GROUP_AD_WANT` and `GROUP_AD`. A `GROUP_AD_WANT` names the wanted group in the envelope's `group` and carries an empty body; a receiver serves it to non-members exactly as it serves a `PING`, under the bootstrap rate budget. A `GROUP_AD` answers with the signed founding advertisement (§5.1) the answering member itself joined with; a member serves only advertisements that are self-certifying founding documents, and the requester re-derives the GroupID and checks the founder's signature before it acts on one, ignoring every answer that fails. Earlier drafts also listed `DIGEST_DIFF`, `PULL`, `PIPE_OPEN`, and `PIPE_CLOSE`; the `DIGEST`/`PULL_RESP` pair and bare `PIPE_DATA` realize them and the four names are retired.

The wrapper's sender key and signature are optional as of v0.1.6: both present is a signed frame, both absent is a channel-attested frame acceptable only on a connection attested for the sender (§5.6), and any other combination is dropped. Hybrid logical clocks timestamp all items. Receivers reject envelopes whose signature fails verification or whose group the receiver does not belong to, and they deduplicate gossip items by stream and payload hash (§5.3). The QUIC (RFC 9000) binding ships in v0.1.4, carrying these envelopes over one bidirectional stream per peer connection; a refinement mapping capability pipes to QUIC streams one-to-one remains future work. The protocol thus rests on standards-based interfaces and formats throughout: CBOR encoding, Ed25519 signatures, and URI naming, with nothing Java-specific on the wire.

## 10. The Embabel Extension: `embabel-agentspaces`

The first implementation ships as an Embabel/Spring Boot starter. The design intent: an Embabel developer gets P2P coordination by adding a dependency and a few properties, with zero new mental models beyond "the blackboard can now be shared, and shared state is leased."

### 10.1 Bootstrapping

Adding the starter dependency (`agentspaces-spring-boot-starter`) is the whole enablement step. Spring Boot auto-configuration starts the peer, joins the configured groups, attaches the configured spaces, and enrolls annotated beans. Earlier drafts showed an `@EnableAgentSpaces` annotation; it does not exist, and the starter dependency plus the `agentspaces.*` properties replace it, because auto-configuration is the idiomatic Spring Boot form of the same intent. A group is identified in one of two mutually exclusive ways. `founding` names a string every member turns into the same GroupID locally, for fleets whose members share configuration. `join` names an existing self-certifying group by its `aspace://<groupID>` URI (or bare GroupID) and requires `seeds`: the peer asks the seeds for the founding advertisement with `GROUP_AD_WANT`, verifies the first `GROUP_AD` answer against the GroupID it holds (§5.1), and joins with it, failing after `join-timeout-millis` (default 10,000) when no seed served a verified document. When neither is set, the group name doubles as the founding string.

Under the starter, every auto-configured space publishes its SpaceAdvertisement on creation and rides a per-group block exchange, so bulk payloads are content-addressed without further wiring (§7.5); a configured `content-key` seals every space in the group and the key-wrap capability serves it to admitted members (§11a); annotated beans bind into every group whose spaces satisfy them, with one card per group (§10.3, §10.4); and `@ProvidesCapability` beans are registered through the bean post-processor (§10.5). The `agentspaces.transport.*`, `agentspaces.security.*`, `agentspaces.crypto.*`, `agentspaces.console.*`, and `agentspaces.a2a.*` groups configure the channel authentication of §5.6, the security profile, the signature provider, the fleet console, and the A2A gateway of §12, and are documented with their modules.

`agentspaces.security.profile` selects the security posture and, with it, the authorizer of §11. Under `dev-local` and `mtls` the starter builds one membership authorizer per networked group and reads its optional grants from `agentspaces.security.grants.{raft-voter, directive-issuer, connector-serve, key-holder, key-rotator, space-write, space-take, vote, model-serve}`, each a list of PeerIDs where an empty list permits any admitted member, except `key-rotator`, where an empty list leaves rotation to the founder alone (§11a.3). Under `mtls-oidc` and `zero-trust` it builds one OIDC authorizer from `agentspaces.security.oidc.{issuer, audience, jwks-url}`, fails at startup naming the missing property when any is absent, and ignores the grants. The node's own token comes from `agentspaces.security.oidc.token` or `token-file`, which the starter re-reads on every card-refresh tick so a sidecar can rotate it, and travels in the `aspace:oidc` hint of the node's self-advertisement; a configured token that fails validation at startup logs a warning rather than failing, since the identity provider may be briefly unreachable. When the console's command and control is enabled, the `directive-issuer` grant becomes the configured issuers plus this node, so worker nodes list the console's PeerID under `grants.directive-issuer`. A space with `admission: credential` takes its issuer from `credential-issuer`, defaulting to this node; a space with `admission: authorizer` uses the group's authorizer; and a configured `content-key` is served through the same authorizer under `KEY_HOLDER`. The console's command routes use the static `agentspaces.console.command.token` when it is set; when it is blank and `agentspaces.security.oidc.*` is configured, the console validates identity-provider JWTs per request and requires `agentspaces.console.command.required-scope` (default `aspace:console:operate`) of them (§12).

*Multicast beacon (roadmap M1, opt-in).* A peer MAY announce itself on a LAN with a UDP multicast beacon. Every interval, on the node's own clock, the peer sends one datagram per joined group carrying only its signed self-advertisement, encoded as `SignedPeerAd{adBytes, publicKey, signature}` with no envelope and no new frame kind, at most 65,507 bytes, with an IP TTL of 1. A receiver spends a bootstrap-bucket token on each datagram, verifies it fully (the key hashes to the issuer, the signature verifies, the advertisement is unexpired and issued within ten minutes of local time), ignores it unless the named group is one the receiver has joined, applies the group's admission policy, and then introduces itself to the advertised endpoints as if they were configured seeds. Under required attestation (§5.6) the datagram admits nobody and serves only as a seed hint. The reference implementation ships the real UDP carrier and tests the beacon through an in-memory datagram fabric; the UDP carrier itself is exercised only by hand.

### 10.2 The space as an injectable, Embabel-style fluent API

```java
public record ResearchTask(String topic, int priority, String requester) {}
public record Finding(String topic, String summary, String cid) {}

@Component
public record Coordinator(AgentSpaces spaces) {

  public void submit(String topic) {
    spaces.group("research-fleet").space("tasks")
          .write(new ResearchTask(topic, 3, "coordinator"),
                 Lease.of(Duration.ofMinutes(30)));
  }

  public List<Finding> findings(String topic) {
    return spaces.group("research-fleet").space("findings")
          .readAll(Template.of(Finding.class).where("topic", eq(topic)), 50);
  }
}
```

The fluent chain (`spaces.group(…).space(…).write(…)`) deliberately mirrors Embabel's `ai.withLlm(…).createObject(…)` style: context narrows left to right, and terminal operations are typed. The API surface follows functional composition, with each step returning a narrower typed context that the terminal operation executes.

### 10.3 Space-triggered actions: the replicated-worker idiom as an annotation

```java
@Agent(description = "Researches topics from the shared task space")
public class ResearchWorker {

  @SpaceRef(group = "research-fleet", space = "findings")
  private Space findings;                             // injected at bind time

  @SpaceTake(group = "research-fleet", space = "tasks",
             lease = "PT10M")                       // TAKE lease; crash ⇒ task reappears
  @Action
  public Finding research(ResearchTask task) {
    String summary = summarize(task.topic());       // the action body is ordinary code
    return new Finding(task.topic(), summary, null); // return ⇒ complete(take, result)
  }
}
```

`@SpaceTake` subscribes the method's parameter type as a take-template; the method takes exactly one parameter, the entry, and reaches spaces through `@SpaceRef` fields. The runtime performs the leased take under the space's conflict strategy, invokes the action, and on normal return atomically `complete`s the take and writes the result entry (into the take space by default, or the space named as `resultSpace`). An exception or crash simply lets the lease lapse, and the task reappears for another worker. This delivers the JavaSpaces master-worker pattern as one annotation on an ordinary Embabel action, and it complements Embabel's own workflow orchestration: the planner still sequences actions within each agent while the space distributes work across the fleet.

The `group` attribute (v0.1.10) on `@SpaceTake`, `@SpaceNotify`, `@BidFunction`, and `@SpaceRef` scopes a binding when a peer belongs to several groups. An empty `group` means every group whose registered spaces satisfy the binding, and `AgentSpaces.bind(bean)` binds the bean into each of them; a named group means that group alone. A named group the facade has not joined, or a binding no group can satisfy, fails fast with an argument error. A `@SpaceRef` without a group is injected by every binding group, the last one to bind winning, so an agent that spans groups names the group on its references. A space consults one cost function, so there is one `@BidFunction` per space per peer: a second bidder bound on the same peer for the same space fails fast, naming both agents, rather than silently replacing the first (v0.1.11, QA4 A4-8). The binder is told how an agent's signing identity is made (v0.1.11, QA4 A4-7 phase 3): by default every bound agent signs with the peer key and is attributed on the peer's word, exactly as before; with a subordinate identity factory — the starter's `agentspaces.identity.agent-keys=subordinate`, or the facade's third constructor argument — each bound agent receives a peer-certified key of its own, its `@SpaceRef` fields and its take and notify loops act through per-agent views of the registered spaces (§4.2), its records are `AGENT_ATTESTED`, and its AgentCard carries the key (§6.1). No annotation changes; the agent classes hold no identity code. Layer 4 has annotations of the same shape (v0.1.11): `@Ballot` — the method takes the vote's `Proposal` and returns the option, cast as the bound agent once per proposal (`null` abstains), the cue being the proposal entry itself so a ballot is never refused as unknown; `@OnDecision` — the method takes the `Decision` and runs once per proposal when this replica's tally meets the quorum, a returned value being written as the next entry; `@OrderedTake` — `@SpaceTake`'s contract with the take routed through the space's registered ordered-log coordinator (§7.4, §8); `@CapabilityRef` — a typed capability client injected by field type; and a `Contribution(epochId, value)` return from a bound method, which starts the peer's aggregate epoch instead of writing an entry (§8). Each fails fast at bind time when its capability is not registered for the space, naming the registration. `bind(agent, name)` binds an instance under an explicit agent name, so several instances of one class are several agents.

The `description` attribute (v0.1.13) on `@SpaceTake`, `@SpaceNotify`, and `@OrderedTake` describes that one action. The binder publishes it as the action's description on the agent's card (§6.1); empty means the card's agent description stands in. Every bound method — including `@Ballot` and `@OnDecision` — becomes one declared action, and an Embabel `@Action` contributes its own `description` the same way (§10.4).

Its choreography sibling, `@SpaceNotify` (completed in v0.1.5), invokes the method on matching writes without consuming them, and a non-void return value is written back as the next entry in the flow, so react-to-X-produce-Y choreography is one returning method. The binder carries the choreography discipline the method body would otherwise need: delivery is deduplicated per entry (at-least-once becomes effectively-once per bound agent), the reaction runs on its own virtual thread so slow work never holds the fabric's delivery thread, a null return writes nothing, and the binder renews the subscription lease while the agent stays bound.

v0.1.5 also removes the remaining ceremony from all of the binding annotations. The `space` attribute is optional everywhere: an empty name resolves to the binder's sole registered space, so the common one-space agent names no spaces at all (several spaces without a name fail fast, listing the candidates). Duration attributes accept Spring-style simple strings (`"10m"`, `"500ms"`, `"30s"`) alongside ISO-8601. A `@SpaceRef` field of type `Space` is injected at bind time, giving method bodies a handle for mid-flight writes (progress markers, side outputs) with no facade plumbing, and, under Spring, making `@Scheduled` plus `@SpaceRef` the idiomatic periodic agent. Finally, the binder resolves the class-level `@AgentSpec` through one level of meta-annotation: any annotation itself annotated `@AgentSpec` acts as a composed stereotype whose same-named attributes (`name`, `description`, `goals`) supply the identity. The Spring starter ships `@SpaceAgent` built exactly this way, a stereotype that is both `@Component` and `@AgentSpec`, so the whole Spring experience is one annotation on the class and one on the method:

```java
@SpaceAgent(description = "Researches topics from the shared task space")
public class Researcher {

  @SpaceTake                                   // sole space: inferred; lease default "10m"
  public Finding research(ResearchTask task) {
    return new Finding(task.topic(), summarize(task));
  }
}
```

For cost-aware allocation, the bid is a method:

```java
@SpaceTake(group = "research-fleet", space = "tasks-auction")   // AUCTION space
@Action
public Finding research(ResearchTask t) { … }

@BidFunction(group = "research-fleet", space = "tasks-auction")
public double bid(ResearchTask t) {                 // lower = cheaper = selected
  return estimatedTokens(t) * modelCostPerToken() + queueDepthPenalty();
}
```

This follows the functional programming style Embabel already encourages: the bid policy is a plain function the application supplies, and the runtime composes it into the auction rounds.

*Tags, tagged returns, and the fan-in (v0.1.14).* `@SpaceTake`, `@SpaceNotify`, and `@OrderedTake` accept `tags = {"key=value", "key"}`, compiled onto the method's template as tag conditions (§7.2) so a filtered take never claims an entry it would not serve. A bound method MAY return `Tagged.of(entry, tags)`; the binder unwraps it and writes the entry with those tags, atomically with the completion for a same-space take result, and the card declares the wrapped type. `@SpaceJoin` is the fan-in as one annotation: the method takes a `Joined` bag and is invoked once per key when every required `@Part` (an entry type, the field or tag that carries its key, optional tag filters, optionally another space, optionally marked optional) is present. The binder subscribes to each part, seeds its per-key state from the space on bind and again for any key it meets for the first time, re-reads the parts by key when it fires, and forgets a key whose parts stop arriving after `within`. The mode says how often one key may fire. `LOCAL`: once per key per bound agent, from an in-memory set. `LEASED`: the binder writes a `JoinTicket(join, key)` entry (§7.1, tagged `join` and `key`) when the key completes, withdraws it when an older ticket for the key is already visible, and fires from a leased take of tickets, once per lease race per ticket. `ORDERED`: the ticket is taken through the space's ordered-log coordinator and every member records the committed claims in the same order, so the first committed ticket per key is the only one that fires, fleet-wide. A join declares one card action of kind `join` consuming every part's schema; it is never invocable by writing one entry. A binder MAY be given the schema registry its spaces use, so cards, remote actions, and records agree on every type name under a custom naming scheme.

*Opening a vote (v0.1.14, ISSUE-Motion and ISSUE-Propose).* A bound method MAY return a `Motion(space, proposalId, question, options, quorum, lease)`: the binder opens a QUORUM proposal on the vote capability registered for the named (or sole) vote space, as the bound agent, once per proposal id per replica (a motion whose proposal is already visible is skipped), and completes a take before opening so a failed open never makes a finished task reappear; a method that declares a `Motion` or a `Contribution` return is refused at bind time when the capability it needs is not registered. `@Propose` is the annotation form for a lead whose shape is fixed: the method takes the cue and returns the question (`null` asks nothing; a `Motion` is allowed for the odd cue), and the annotation carries the vote space, the proposal id rule (a prefix plus the cue's key fields joined by `:`, or a cue tag), the options, the quorum, the lease, and tag filters on the cue. The binder opens at most once per proposal id per bound agent (several cues may name one proposal) and per replica, as the bound agent, and declares one card action of kind `propose`, never invocable. A class MAY both propose and vote on one space; attribution and the authorizer decide what counts.

*The workflow verbs (v0.1.14, ISSUE-WorkflowVerbs).* The remaining shapes a workflow takes are the same two moves, a method and an entry, with the shape declared on the annotation. *Fork.* A bound method MAY return `Entries.of(...)`: the binder completes a take once and then dispatches each element exactly as if the method had returned it alone, so an element MAY be an entry, a `Tagged` entry, a `Motion`, or a `Contribution`; a nested `Entries` is refused. A card cannot see inside a collection, so `@SpaceTake`, `@SpaceNotify`, `@OrderedTake`, and `@SpaceJoin` accept `produces = {Type.class, ...}`, which the card lists for that action. *Route.* `where = {"field=value", "field!=value"}` on the same annotations, on `@Part`, and on `@Propose` compiles onto the method's template as field conditions, judged before the method is invoked and before a take claims, with the value compared as its string form; a filter that names a component the parameter type lacks, or that is malformed, is refused at bind time naming the method. A method whose declared return is a sealed interface has every permitted subclass listed on its card as produced, and the concrete one is written. *Gather.* A `@Part` MAY appear several times for one key: `atLeast = n` waits for a fixed number, `countedBy = "Type.field"` waits for the number another part of the same join declares in an integer field, read whenever that part lands, before or after the counted ones; `settle` on the join fires once the key has been quiet for that long and every minimum holds; `Joined.all(Type.class)` hands every gathered element over; a join with one part is allowed when it gathers. *Deadline.* `@SpaceNotify(on = {...})` selects the event kinds a reaction answers (`WRITTEN` by default, any set of §7.2's kinds, an empty set refused at bind time), so a leased entry is a timer: a reaction on `EXPIRED` runs when the lease lapses, at every replica, which is why such a reaction pairs with a `Motion` return or an idempotent write. *Settled estimate.* `@OnEstimate(prefix, settleTicks, tolerance)` reacts once per push-sum epoch when the estimate has stayed within the tolerance for that many consecutive protocol ticks (§8); the method takes the capability's `Estimate` and its return is dispatched as every other bound method's; the card declares one action of kind `on-estimate`, never invocable; binding is refused when no aggregate is registered. None of the six changes the wire, the codec, the spaces' matching, or any capability's exchange. *Reduce (v0.1.15, ISSUE-SpaceReduce).* `@SpaceReduce` binds a method `A apply(A accumulator, E element)`: the binder takes one element under a lease, invokes the method with the key's current accumulator (`null` on the first step), and completes the element with the new accumulator as the result in the same space, so an element is never folded twice; a `JoinTicket` per key, taken before a drain and completed after it, serializes the steps of one key, through the space's claim race (`LEASED`) or the ordered log (`ORDERED`), and a reducer whose ticket is not the key's oldest live one backs off. The accumulator carries the tags `reduce`, `key`, and `steps` and is written under `accumulatorLease`, the key's idle timeout: a quiet key lapses and its lapse is an `EXPIRED` event. The return is the accumulator, a `Tagged` one, or an `Entries` fork holding exactly one accumulator and side outputs written after it; `null` consumes the element and changes nothing. The card declares one action of kind `reduce`, never invocable. Nothing new travels: the ticket is the reserved type of v0.1.14 and the accumulator is the application's own record.

### 10.4 Automatic AgentCards

At startup the extension reflects over registered Embabel agents, reading their `@Agent` descriptions, goals, and action input and output types, and publishes one AgentCard per agent into every joined group, leased and refreshed together with the facade's other cards. A card's space bindings (§6.1) are filled from the agent's `@SpaceTake` and `@SpaceNotify` methods, the take binding winning when both name the same consumed type, so the card states which space each input type is taken from. A bean that is both an Embabel agent and a space-agent stereotype receives one card, from the space binder. Other peers can therefore discover which agent can produce a `Finding` from a `ResearchTask` with no additional code, and the semantic-discovery capability can match on the natural-language descriptions Embabel already requires.

### 10.5 Capability services as Spring beans

Providers implement the `CapabilityProvider` SPI and annotate the bean `@ProvidesCapability`; the facade registers the bean on the named group's capability runtime, or on every joined group's when the annotation names none, and the runtime publishes and refreshes its advertisement (§8). Consumers ask the group context for a typed client: `spaces.group("research-fleet").capability(VoteClient.class).propose(…)`. Clients come from the `CapabilityClientFactory<C>` SPI, discovered through `ServiceLoader` and extendable with `AgentSpaces.clientFactory(...)`, and the facade caches one client per group and client type. The shipped `VoteClient` uses a vote capability hosted in the same process when one exists and otherwise builds over the vote space a discovered advertisement's `space:<name>` binding names, which requires the group to have registered that space; the shipped `AggregateClient` wraps, or creates, the group's push-sum aggregate over its capability pipes. `refreshCards()` refreshes the capability advertisements in the same pass as the cards, and under the starter `card-refresh-millis` drives both for every group. `agentspaces.capabilities.*` toggles the shipped providers: `enabled` switches the whole surface; `aggregate`, `vote`, `semantic-discovery` (the deterministic hashing embedder), and `key-wrap` (which serves the group's configured content key) default on; `ordered-log` defaults off, and turning it on fails fast, because a Raft log needs a fixed member set the starter cannot infer, so a deployment supplies its own `@ProvidesCapability` bean; `gossip-learn` defaults off and, when on, wires gossip learning with the default weight-averaging model. The vote provider tallies over the space `votes-space` names (default `votes`): a configured group space of that name is used as it stands, and otherwise the provider creates a private LEASE_RACE space that it does not register with the group, so sole-space inference for the application's own bindings is unaffected. The LEASE_RACE and AUCTION strategies are space configuration and need no toggle.

### 10.6 Remote AgentCards as planner actions (v0.1.4)

Section 10.4 sends the fleet's skills out as AgentCards; this section brings them back in as actions. The remote-actions bridge closes the loop the §14 open question described: a planner treats the whole fleet's advertised capabilities as its own action space, so cross-peer plans are first-class.

The framework-neutral half lives in the core agent module as a registry (`RemoteActions`). It watches the group's ad-cache for foreign AgentCards, never the peer's own, and exposes one remote action per card and (consumed, produced) schema pair whose types resolve locally: for a card that declares its actions (§6.1, v0.1.13), the pairs of its invocable actions (`take`, `notify`, `ordered-take`, `embabel-action`; ballots and decision reactions are not invoked by writing an entry), so a two-action agent yields exactly two remote actions; for an older card, the cross product of its flat lists. A remote action's name stays `<agent>_<InputType>` so planners' action names are stable, and it exposes the declared action and prefers that action's description. A remote action carries the card's name, description, goals, and cost hints, its typed input and output, and one operation: invoke, which writes the input entry into the space the remote workers take that type from, then awaits a result entry that correlates with the request. Correlation defaults to shared-field matching: record components with the same name in request and result must hold equal values, the convention every AgentSpaces example already uses (a `Finding` carries the `topic` of its `ResearchTask`), and a registry may replace the predicate. Routing resolves the input's space in a fixed precedence: an explicit route declared on the registry, then the card's own space binding (§6.1) when it names a space this peer has registered, then the sole registered space. A one-space group therefore needs no routes at all, and a card that declares its bindings needs none in a multi-space group either. Results are awaited in the input's space unless a `resultsIn` route names another, matching `@SpaceTake`'s own result-space default.

The semantics are deliberately choreography. The invocation is an ordinary signed, leased entry; the card names who advertised the capability, but any qualified member may serve the request, and a worker that crashes mid-task lets its take lease lapse so another finishes the job, and the awaited result still arrives, from whoever completed it. An invocation nobody serves times out empty with the entry still leased in the space, a work item awaiting a future worker. And because cards are TTL-leased, the action space is live: an agent that stops refreshing its card ages out of the planner's repertoire exactly as it ages out of discovery.

The Embabel half generates what the GOAP planner needs: a class annotated `@Agent` with one typed `@Action` method per remote action (bytecode-generated, since the planner conditions on real method signatures), each method delegating to its remote action's invoke, with card goals surfaced as `@AchievesGoal` so goal conditions see remote capabilities too. A result that never arrives within the configured timeout throws, which the planner treats as the action failing. The generated agent deploys onto the Embabel platform reflectively, so the bridge compiles with no Embabel artifact, exactly like the rest of the extension.

*Auto-deploy (v0.1.10).* A deployer keeps the platform's view of the fleet current. Once started with a platform, it polls the registry on a virtual thread and deploys a freshly generated agent whenever the set of remote actions changes: when the first cards arrive, when a new card brings a new capability, and when an expired card takes one away. A card refresh that changes only the issue time triggers no redeploy. The Embabel platform surface this module reaches has no undeploy, so a vanished action leaves the next generated agent while the earlier generation stays registered until the platform restarts, and invoking one of its vanished actions times out and throws, which the planner treats as the action failing. Under Spring Boot the bridge is configured with `agentspaces.embabel.*` properties: `remote-actions-enabled` (default true, and the bridge bean exists only when it is true), `auto-deploy` (default true), which wires the deployer as a `SmartLifecycle` over the `AgentPlatform` bean, `deploy-poll-millis` (default 2,000) for the registry poll, and the remote-action timeout and the generated agent's name and description as knobs.

The enablement property this buys, verified by integration test: an agent whose local actions cannot chain to a goal type provably finds no plan alone, and finds the multi-hop plan through remote specialists the moment the fleet's cards are bridged in, then executes it as leased space round-trips across live peers.

## 11. Security Considerations

Signatures on every advertisement and entry provide integrity and attribution, and group membership policy forms the perimeter. Open groups are Sybil-exposed by construction; deployments that need Sybil resistance must use INVITE or POLICY groups with credentials, since the core deliberately includes no proof-of-work or proof-of-stake machinery. Replay is bounded by TTL windows, per-item deduplication keyed by stream and payload hash (so a pre-seeded identifier cannot suppress an honest item), and a bounded-drift hybrid logical clock: a received timestamp cannot pull a node's clock more than a fixed skew (ten minutes) ahead of local wall time, so a peer cannot poison the total order that take arbitration depends on, and the same ceiling bounds how far a take claim may be back-dated (§7.4), so a peer cannot win every race by presenting an old stamp. A receiver also drops frames of any wire version other than its own before signature verification (§9). For encryption, v0.1 relies on transport-level protection (TLS 1.3 per RFC 8446, or Noise); §11a specifies the group-keyed payload encryption and sealed key distribution that close this gap, so that rendezvous and relay peers outside a subgroup cannot read its traffic, and §11a.4 authenticates the mutable state transitions that the record signature alone does not cover. Per-issuer inbound rate limits, a mandatory payload-size cutover to content addressing, and lease-capped cache occupancy bound resource exhaustion from advertisement floods and giant payloads. v0.1 explicitly leaves four threats unaddressed, stated plainly so deployments can plan around them: Byzantine manipulation of AUCTION bids, where a lying bidder secures work it will never perform (reputation entries and lease-lapse re-auction provide an operational mitigation; the core offers no cryptographic defense); eclipse attacks on bootstrap (multiple seed sources provide the mitigation); poisoning of the push-sum aggregate and gossip-learning values, where any participant can contribute an arbitrary value to an unauthenticated average, which is the stated cost of cheap approximate aggregation, so trust-sensitive decisions must use QUORUM voting instead, whose per-ballot writer authentication makes the tally recomputable and auditable; and, under group content keys (§11a.1), the plaintext `type` and `tags` fields, which remain readable by keyless relays and rendezvous peers so that matching and sharding keep working; schema names and tag values are therefore visible metadata, and payloads that must not leak shape belong behind neutral type names and tags. v0.1.13 accepts a fifth: the back-dating window of signing-time judgement (§4.2). Because a receiver judges an agent certificate at the signing time a record or state claims, a stolen agent key can sign with an old stamp that falls inside a certificate window that was valid then, and the receiver accepts it; revocation with reason `key-compromise` (§6.1) is the remedy, since it refuses every newly arriving signature by the key whatever its claimed signing time.

**Authorization (v0.1.11).** Membership decides who is inside the perimeter; the `Authorizer` decides what a member may do. Every privileged decision in the fabric is one question, `permits(peer, operation, scope)`, over nine operations. The ordered-log Raft quorum asks `RAFT_VOTER` with the group id as scope for every inbound Raft frame, which must also come from the fixed member set, and for every client submission, so a member the authorizer refuses never votes, leads, or submits. A worker's directive gate asks `DIRECTIVE_ISSUER` for the space-authenticated issuer of each directive, with the group id as scope under the starter or whatever scope the attaching code names. The key-wrap holder asks `KEY_HOLDER` with the group id for each requester (§11a.2). A data-query client asks `CONNECTOR_SERVE` with the asset name for the space-authenticated issuer of each result it would trust, and a connector asks the same question about itself and refuses to advertise an asset it may not serve. A space under `AUTHORIZER` admission asks `SPACE_WRITE` and `SPACE_TAKE` with the space name, per transition (§7.5). A QUORUM tally asks `VOTE` with the vote space's name for the authenticated issuer of every ballot it would count, and a voter asks the same question about itself before casting, so a ballot that no tally would count is never written (§8). A caller of a fleet model service asks `MODEL_SERVE` (v0.1.12) with the answering model's name for the space-authenticated issuer of every model response and stream chunk it would accept, so only permitted model servers can answer, and a model server asks the same question about itself for each model it serves. A member asks `KEY_ROTATE` (v0.1.13) with the group id before it rotates the group content key and before it installs an epoch key minted by anyone but the founder (§11a.3); unlike the other operations, it permits nobody who holds no explicit grant. The security profile selects the answerer. Under `dev-local` and `mtls` the answerer is a membership authorizer per group: every admitted member is permitted unless the deployment names grants for an operation (§10.1), in which case only the listed identities are. A grant list names PeerIDs or AgentIDs (`peer/localName`; v0.1.11, QA4 A4-7 phase 2). A bare PeerID admits every agent on that peer; an AgentID names one agent and puts the operation at `AGENT` granularity, under which an agent must be named to be permitted while its peer, asked at peer level, is permitted because it hosts a granted agent. An authorizer answers `permits(agent, operation, scope)` per agent where it holds agent-level grants and by delegating to the agent's peer otherwise, and reports its `granularity(operation, scope)` so consumers that count identities (§8 QUORUM) count at the granularity they authorize at. A grant never outlives its peer's admission. Under `mtls-oidc` and `zero-trust` the answerer is an OIDC authorizer that validates identity-provider JWTs against the configured issuer, audience, and JWKS, binds each token to one PeerID through its `agentspaces_peer` claim, and reads the permitted operations from the token's scopes, `aspace:<operation>` for every scope of an operation and `aspace:<operation>:<scope>` for one; a token binds to one PeerID, so the OIDC authorizer speaks at `PEER` granularity and every agent on the peer inherits its token's scopes (a per-agent token claim is a stated follow-on, not implied); a deployment on these profiles that names no issuer, audience, or JWKS URL fails at startup. A peer presents its token to every peer that will judge it through the channel join credentials already use: the token rides in the peer's signed self-advertisement under the resource hint `aspace:oidc`, each receiving node hands a member's hints to the authorizer on first admission and whenever they change, and a peer refreshes its token by updating its own hint. Two consequences follow and are accepted: the token adds its size, typically under 2 KiB, to the self-advertisement that gossips once per period; and because the token names one PeerID, a hint replayed from one advertisement into another grants nothing to the other peer. Every enforcement point also keeps a membership-rooted construction path, so a fleet that names no authorizer behaves as it did before this revision. Each group's authorizer is scoped to that group (v0.1.13): whatever the group has revoked, peers and agents alike (§6.1), is refused before the answerer is asked, and under the OIDC profiles, whose authorizer one node shares across all its groups, a peer must also be a member of the group, since a valid token is otherwise valid in every group the node joins.

**11a. The security addendum (v0.1.2, normative).** This addendum specifies the mechanisms the v0.1 draft deferred, all now implemented in the reference implementation.

**11a.1 Group content keys.** A group MAY protect entry payloads with a symmetric group content key: AES-256-GCM with a fresh 96-bit random nonce per encryption, the sealed form `nonce || ciphertext || tag`, and associated data binding each ciphertext to its entry as the UTF-8 of `spaceId + "|" + entryId`, so a sealed payload cannot be replayed under another entry identity. From v0.1.13 a group's content keys form a ring of numbered epochs (§11a.3): epoch 0 is the configured key, and a record sealed under a later epoch names it in the appended record field `keyEpoch` (absent for epoch 0, so records sealed under the configured key are byte-identical to earlier revisions) and appends it to the associated data, `spaceId + "|" + entryId + "|" + epoch`, so a ciphertext cannot be relabelled to another epoch. Writers encrypt before signing, and the record signature therefore covers the ciphertext and the epoch. This ordering is what keeps topology helpers useful without trusting them: a member without the key still verifies every signature, stores every record, and forwards every delta, and it can read none of the payloads. Key possession, per space, is the read perimeter; group membership remains the write perimeter.

**11a.2 Sealed key distribution (`aspace:cap/key-wrap`).** Key holders distribute the content key per member through an ECIES-style seal. Each peer holds an X25519 (RFC 7748) decryption keypair alongside its Ed25519 signing pair, so the signing key never enters any decryption path. A requester sends its raw X25519 public key inside a signed envelope; the holder authenticates the requester from the envelope (the self-certifying PeerID), applies its authorization policy to that verified identity, and answers with the key wrapped as: a fresh ephemeral X25519 keypair per wrap, an RFC 7748 agreement with the recipient key, HKDF (RFC 5869, HMAC-SHA-256) salted with both public keys followed by the SHA-256 of the exchange binding, under the label `aspace-group-key-wrap-v2`, and AES-256-GCM over the key material with that binding digest as associated data. The binding is the canonical CBOR of `(group, holder, requester, nonce)`: the request carries a 64-bit random nonce, the requester accepts a response only from the holder it asked, and only under the binding it expects, so a wrap made for any other exchange does not open and no third party can complete or deny a request (v0.1.13, ASF-046). Only the asker's decryption key opens the reply, so the response can travel any path the fabric offers, relays included. Under a ring (§11a.3) a request names the epochs it wants and the response carries one wrap per granted epoch, each with its rotator, cutover, and rotator proof, and each bound with the appended binding fields `epoch` and `rotator`; a requester installs an epoch key only when the proof verifies (§11a.3). Authorization stays a deployment policy (an allowlist, a membership check, a credential validation); the capability enforces the policy it is given and nothing else (P6).

**11a.2a Per-agent key wrap (v0.1.13, normative).** An agent with a subordinate key (§4.2) MAY hold its own X25519 keypair, certified by its peer: the AgentCertificate body appends `encryptionPublicKey` (32 raw bytes, absent otherwise, so certificates without it are byte-identical to earlier revisions). Such an agent asks for epoch keys for itself: the request appends `agent = (agent, certificate, peerKey)`, the encoded AgentID, its current certificate, and its peer's raw Ed25519 key. The holder MUST serve it only when all of these hold: the peer key hashes to the authenticated sender's PeerID; the AgentID names an agent of that peer; the certificate verifies under that key for exactly that agent and covers the holder's present time (§4.2); it certifies an encryption key; the agent is not revoked; and the holder's policy admits the agent itself (the `Authorizer`'s per-agent `KEY_HOLDER` decision, §11), whatever it would decide for the peer. The holder seals each wrap to the certified encryption key, never to a key named elsewhere in the request, and binds it with the appended binding field `agent`. A peer that is not itself a key holder can therefore host an agent that is, and a sibling agent on the same peer learns nothing from it. The peer key remains the unit of possession for peer-level requests, which are unchanged.

**11a.3 Content-key rotation (v0.1.13, normative).** A rotation mints the next epoch: a fresh content key, a cutover instant, and a proof, the rotator's Ed25519 signature over the canonical CBOR of `(group, epoch, cutover, sha-256(key))` together with the rotator's raw public key. Rotation authority is the group founder or a holder of the `KEY_ROTATE` grant in the group's scope, judged by each member under its own configuration (so every member is configured with the same grants, as with `RAFT_VOTER`); a peer without it MUST refuse to rotate, and every member MUST refuse to install an epoch key whose proof does not verify for an authorized rotator, whichever holder relayed it. Epoch 0 is the configured key and carries no proof. The `key-wrap` advertisement of a holder publishes `holder`, `epochs` (the newest 64 it holds), `epoch` (its newest), and that epoch's `cutover`; a member follows these advertisements, records the cutover of an epoch announced by an authorized rotator, and fetches any epoch it lacks from a holder that lists it, as it does when it meets a record naming an epoch it does not hold. Readers open a record with the key of the epoch it names. Writers seal under the newest held epoch whose cutover has passed; a writer that knows a newer epoch has cut over but does not yet hold its key keeps sealing under the older one for a writer grace (one hour by default) after that cutover, and past it refuses to seal, so no writer extends a retired epoch indefinitely. Two authorized rotators may race to mint the same epoch number: a ring keeps every verified key for an epoch, opens with whichever authenticates, and seals with the canonical one, the key whose rotator's hex SHA-256 of `epoch + "|" + rotator` sorts lowest. Epoch keys are retained, not pruned, because write leases renew without limit and an entry sealed under any epoch may still be live; a ring holds at most 4096 epochs. A peer with a keystore persists its ring at `<keystore>/content-keys/<group-id>.ring`, each key sealed to the peer's own persisted X25519 key under a binding naming the group, epoch, and rotator. Rotation does not re-encrypt existing entries: a lease renewal keeps the epoch the record was sealed under, and only a new write seals under the current epoch. Random 96-bit GCM nonces bound one content key to roughly 2^32 encryptions before collision risk becomes material, and that budget is fleet-wide: every writer seals under the same epoch key and no node can count another's seals. A group MUST therefore rotate on time, at a period by which the whole fleet's write rate stays well below that ceiling; the reference implementation rotates on a schedule (`content-key-rotation.rotate-every`) or on demand, and each node additionally counts its own seals per key, warns at 2^31, and refuses past 2^32. Rotation bounds what a removed member can read in future: an agent or peer revoked or no longer granted is refused every epoch minted after its removal (§11a.2a), while entries sealed under epochs it already held stay readable to it.

**11a.4 Authenticated state transitions (v0.1.3, normative).** The record signature (§7.1) covers only an entry's immutable identity, which leaves the mutable CRDT fields a delta carries, the completion flag, the observed-remove set, and the lease register, unauthenticated. Without a second signature, any admitted member could replay a victim's validly-signed record with the completion flag set, or with its add-dots copied into removes, or with a far-past lease, and every replica would merge it, making the entry vanish fleet-wide with no attribution. AgentSpaces therefore signs the mutable state as well: every state delta carries a signature over `(spaceId, entryId, sorted adds, sorted removes, lease stamp, lease value, completed)`, and the receiver verifies it before merging. The authorized signer is implied by the transition, so no additional identity travels on the wire: a completed state must be signed by the holder of a valid take claim on the entry (the claim, carried in or already merged from the same exchange, names the holder), and every other state, an initial write, a renewal, a cancellation, by the entry's issuer. This holds whatever decided the claim: a claim that arrived through the ordered log (§7.4) is stored with the holder's own attestation, never with a proof signed by the replica that installed it, since only the holder's key can authenticate the holder's completion; a claim installed without that attestation decides local arbitration and nothing else. A delta whose mutable state is not signed by the party authorized for its transition is dropped. Anti-entropy forwards the stored signature rather than re-deriving one, since a relay is not the author. For a peer-signed party the signing key is the peer's Ed25519 key. For an agent-attested party (a record carrying an agent certificate, or a claim carrying a holder certificate, §4.2) the transition MUST be agent-signed (v0.1.13): the delta appends `signer`, the party's AgentID, `signedAt`, an HLC stamp, and `stateCertificate`, the party's certificate covering `signedAt`; the signed view appends `signer` and `signedAt` (both omitted when absent, so a peer-signed state's signed bytes are unchanged); and a receiver requires `signer` to name exactly the authorized party, verifies the certificate under that party's peer key at `signedAt` (the §4.2 rule), and verifies the state under the certificate's agent key. A peer-signed state for an agent-attested party is refused, as is one naming any other signer. The ordered log carries the holder's certificate in its claim commands, and installs and judges them with the same claim verifier gossip uses. Completion attribution is thus cryptographic: the fleet can prove which agent completed which entry, which is what the coordination-state audit story (an operator reading the space rather than a telemetry pipeline) depends on.

## 12. Scope Boundaries

AgentSpaces is not a blockchain. It maintains no global ledger and no tokens, and the ordered-log capability runs a group-scoped Raft quorum without Byzantine fault tolerance; a third party could add a BFT capability.

AgentSpaces does not replace interoperability standards. MCP remains the tool layer beneath an Embabel agent, and an A2A/ANP gateway that translates AgentCards and space entries to Agent Cards and A2A tasks is the intended bridge to foreign agents; the A2A surface (discovery, tasks, streaming, and push notifications) ships today. The gateway serves its cards under an external base URL: a deployment sets `externalBaseUrl` (trailing slash stripped) whenever the gateway sits off loopback or behind a proxy, and the default is `http://<bind host>:<port>`, the hostname when bound by name, the literal address when bound by address, and `localhost` for a wildcard bind. Task state derives from space semantics: a present entry is `submitted`, a taken one `working`, a completed one `completed`, a withdrawn one `canceled`, and an entry that vanished before anyone took it reads `working` until its write lease deadline and `failed` after it, with the status message "no worker took the task before its lease expired"; `failed` is terminal, so a stream or push subscriber receives it as a final event. The gateway measures task deadlines on an injected clock, the system clock by default, so a deployment passes the clock its spaces run on and the two never disagree about a lapsed lease. The A2A surface applies no CORS policy, because A2A clients are servers; browser access goes through the fleet console, whose per-origin allowlist is a console feature.

The console's command routes and the gateway's JSON-RPC surface share one bearer principal model (v0.1.11). A `BearerAuthenticator` turns a presented bearer token into a principal with a subject and a set of scopes. The static shared token, compared in constant time, yields the fixed subject `operator` and no scopes. An identity-provider JWT, validated on every request against the configured issuer, audience, and JWKS, yields its `sub` and the scopes of its `scope` claim; a token whose `scope` claim is missing or blank is refused rather than admitted without scopes. A token the authenticator refuses is answered `401` with `WWW-Authenticate: Bearer`. A principal that carries scopes but lacks the surface's required scope, `aspace:console:operate` on the console and `aspace:a2a:client` on the gateway, is answered `403`. The audit trail records the token subject as the acting operator, and the request body's `operator` field plays no part in attribution, so revoking an operator at the identity provider ends their access when the token lapses and the ledger names who acted. Discovery GETs stay open on both surfaces. The gateway serves its RPC surface without a gate only when no authenticator is configured, which the design accepts only behind the default loopback bind; the console's mutating routes stay off until a gate is configured.

AgentSpaces is not a database. The space holds coordination state and small payloads, while bulk data lives in content-addressed storage that entries reference.

## 13. Roadmap

**M1 (Embabel extension, single group, LAN), delivered.** M1 delivers Layers 0 through 3 with multicast bootstrap (shipped opt-in in v0.1.10, §10.1), the LEASE_RACE strategy, `@SpaceTake` and `@SpaceNotify`, automatic AgentCards, and in-JVM plus TCP transports. The success criterion is the replicated-worker demo: N Embabel apps on a LAN drain a shared task space with kill-tolerance, in under 30 lines of user code.

**M2 (WAN and strategies), delivered.** M2 adds rendezvous and relay roles, seed-peer bootstrap, the AUCTION strategy with `@BidFunction`, the aggregate and vote capabilities, and content-addressed payloads.

**M3 (Hardening and learning), delivered.** M3 adds partial replication by tag shard, group payload encryption, the ordered-log capability, the gossip-learning capability, an A2A gateway prototype, and a non-JVM wire-compatible client. The wire protocol is CBOR plus Ed25519 with nothing Java-specific, so a second-language client also validates the protocol's independence from the JVM.

**M4 (Lifecycle completion and rotation), in progress.** M4 closes the items v0.1.10 left planned. Shipped: per-agent subordinate keys (v0.1.11 and v0.1.13, §4.2), the key rotation protocol and per-agent key wrap (v0.1.13, §11a.2a, §11a.3), the CA-rooted revocation validator as the gossiped fast path in the enterprise-CA mode (v0.1.13, §6.1), and the trilingual golden vectors for the v0.1.10 record fields and frame kinds. Still open: garbage collection of a released space after its longest entry lease (§7.5), single-server Raft membership changes (§8), and an in-suite test of the real UDP multicast carrier (§10.1).

## 14. Open Questions

Three design questions remain open. How should entry schemas evolve across a mixed-version fleet, where incongruent data between writers and readers would otherwise break matching? The leading option places Avro-style writer and reader schemas in the SpaceAdvertisement, so each space documents the shape of data it carries. Should TAKE leases support work-stealing hand-off, in which a slow holder yields to a better bidder before expiry? Finally, should the LEASE_RACE settle-window default stay fixed, or should it adapt from observed gossip convergence? The question earlier drafts carried here, how much of Embabel's planning should see the space, is resolved in v0.1.4 by §10.6: the GOAP planner treats remote AgentCards as available actions through the remote-actions bridge. Its two refinements closed in v0.1.13: cards declare their actions, each pairing what one bound method consumes with what it produces, and each action carries its own description (§6.1).

Beyond the design questions, the following clauses are specified as planned and the implementation does not yet carry them: space garbage collection after the longest entry lease (§7.5); Raft membership changes, single-server or otherwise (§8); and an in-suite exercise of the real UDP multicast carrier (§10.1). The key rotation protocol, per-agent key wrap, and the CA-rooted revocation validator left this list in v0.1.13 (§11a.2a, §11a.3, §6.1); the validator needs no PeerID-to-certificate directory, because the revocation carries the chain and CRL as evidence. The earlier tension in §5.6 between a SIGNED-mode node's announcement and its acceptance of bare frames is resolved in v0.1.10 by the rule stated there.

**v0.1.9 (September 2026).** The revocation-and-rotation addendum, completing §11's containment story. A new advertisement type, `RevocationAdvertisement`, withdraws a peer's identity: authoritative solely because of who signed it (the group founder by default; deployments may substitute an identity-provider or CA-backed authority), gossiped on a dedicated stream with anti-entropy so late joiners converge, and enforced as permanent refusal at dispatch plus eviction from the membership view; its TTL bounds re-gossip and never the withdrawal itself. Identity rotation is a revocation naming a `successor`: self-certifying identities cannot rotate keys in place, so the trust root re-binds the principal to a fresh PeerID and the successor joins like any new peer. The enterprise-CA channel mode binds the PeerID as a certified attribute of a CA-issued channel certificate, attested only when the chain validates to the deployment's anchors with revocation per its policy (cached CRLs offline-tolerant; live OCSP optional); the CA's revocation is then the authoritative eject with the gossiped advertisement as its fast-path cache. On the wire this adds one advertisement type and one gossip stream; no new frame kinds.
